After Loss, the Self Relearns to Belong: A Psychological Reading of Zucchini in My Life as a Zucchini

Apurba Roy · Samiur Prapon

Department of English, Pundra University of Science & Technology
North South University, Bangladesh

Psychology September 2026 18 min read

Abstract

The article looks at Zucchini’s psychological development in Claude Barras’s book My Life as a Zucchini (2016), concentrating on the way in which grief, disruption of attachment, the need for peer belonging, coping mechanisms, self-efficacy, and resilience interact in the process of rebuilding a child’s sense of self. Instead of analysing Zucchini’s behaviour using a diagnostic approach, the study makes use of Piaget’s cognitive-developmental theory, Erikson’s psychosocial theory, Bowlby’s attachment theory, Bandura’s social learning and self-efficacy theories, Lazarus and Folkman’s theory of coping, Masten’s idea of resilience, and Bronfenbrenner’s ecological systems theory. By looking at particular scenes it shows how Zucchini progresses from feelings of fear, guilt and relational insecurity to a state of agency, emotional integration and a sense of belonging. The article maintains that his development should not be seen as the disappearance of grief but rather as a gradual reorganisation of his relational world, and that it is because of reliable care from his caregivers, peer recognition and opportunities for meaningful action that he is able to turn adversity into a renewed ability to trust and attach.

Keywords: Zucchini; child development; attachment; grief; resilience; self-efficacy; peer relationships; film psychology

Introduction

Childhood is generally thought of as the psychological stage at which the self-step by step gains a sense of continuity, security, and belonging. However, this kind of development does not take place in isolation. A child's conception of self is deeply influenced by his or her relationships with caregivers, with peers, and by the social surroundings in which development occurs. If these environments are disrupted by bereavement, neglect, abandonment, or instability, then the child has to meet the developmental challenges under conditions which can seriously undermine the normal expectation of safety. Claude Barras's book My Life as a Zucchini (2016) provides a particularly close portrayal of this situation via the experiences of Icare, who is fondly known as Zucchini, a nine-year-old boy whose childhood is suddenly changed by the death of his mother and his later entry into a children's home.

The film does not simply present Zucchini as a passive victim of the difficulties of his childhood. On the contrary, it shows psychological growth as a continuous process in which grief, attachment, social recognition, a sense of agency, and the feeling of belonging become linked together. Instead of remaining in a state of withdrawal, guilt, yearning for his mother, difficulty in getting used to new surroundings, and uncertainty as to whether other people will stay, he gradually develops friendship, trust, emotional expression, and the ability to imagine a future again. This kind of change is psychologically important since it proves that adaptation does not have to mean the complete disappearance of painful experiences. Adversity can in fact be absorbed into the child's developing sense of self when he comes across relationships and environments that offer enough security, recognition, and opportunities for agency.

The paper therefore focuses on Zucchini mainly in terms of his developmental, social, emotional, and coping processes rather than using psychiatric diagnosis. In order to understand the connection between Zucchini's developing reasoning and his efforts to make sense of his mother's death, Piaget's account of cognitive development is useful; Erikson's psychosocial theory makes it clear to us how he struggled between feelings of vulnerability and the emergence of a sense of competence. Attachment theory, especially the research carried out by Bowlby (1969) and Ainsworth et al. (1978), offers a way of understanding the impact of disrupted care and of subsequent relational consistency on his expectations regarding safety and trust. Bandura's (1977, 1997) theories of social learning and self-efficacy also shed light on his step-by-step transition from a state of helplessness to one of purposeful action, while Lazarus and Folkman's (1984) theory of stress and coping helps to explain the various ways in which he deals with grief and adversity. Lastly, Masten's (2001) idea of resilience and Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological perspective place Zucchini's adjustment in the context of the protective effects of relationships and social environments.

The main point of this analysis is that Zucchini’s psychological development should not be seen as a straightforward recovery from trauma or as merely involving the substitution of one family for another, but rather as a slow process of rebuilding relational security and a sense of self. This positive change in him results from the combination of responsive care, feeling a sense of belonging with peers, engaging in emotional processing, learning through social interaction, and having opportunities to experience himself as capable of carrying out meaningful actions. The film thus implies a significant principle concerning development: children do not simply outgrow adversity; instead, they grow as a result of relationships which enable them to reinterpreted adversity without letting it set the limits of the self.

1. Context and Psychological Profile

At around nine years of age, when Zucchini first enters the film, it can be seen that he is experiencing emotions that seem not entirely appropriate for that age. He has an enormous amount of mental stress placed upon him in a small amount of time- he faces a situation where his parents have died, his entire security network has gone with them, he feels guilt, displacement and loses his entire system of social comfort, security, and support. These instances of desertion within the movie present classic issues related to grief, guilt, longing for a mother, fear of attachment and the feeling of homelessness with people you don't know.

It is important to draw the difference between observation and interpretation. Zucchini states his mother drinks, bakes for him and enjoys himself with her (Barras, 2016, 00:06:46) and also pleads that they go back to his mom's house (00:16:47). We find that he spends the whole night lying awake (00:15:06), and wakes from night terrors calling out his mothers' name and in tears, shouting, 'mom' (00:37:36), and later exclaims 'I think I killed my mom. But I didn't mean to' (00:19:13). All of these are identifiable observations. While they can easily be interpreted as being signs of grief, guilt, longing or insecurity, they may also simply be indicative of how an 8 or 9-year old can react and would not constitute a psychiatric disorder - it is all in time, seriousness, disruption to his life/functioning and professional assessment.

2. Developmental Analysis

The concrete operational stage according to Piaget usually extends from about seven to eleven years of age. At this stage, children develop a better ability to think logically and to reason causally, although they still find it easier to deal with concrete realities than with highly abstract possibilities (Piaget, 1952; Feldman, 2017, pp. 388–389). Zucchini's way of reasoning shows that he has this kind of developmental ability. In saying "I think I killed my mom. But I didn’t mean to" (Barras, 2016, 00:19:13), he separates the consequence of an action from its intention. Moreover, in his description of his mother he uses contrasting pairs—such as beer and mashed potatoes, fear and fun—instead of making a fully black-and-white judgment (00:06:46).

However, when Raymond tells him that his mother is gone, Zucchini protests by saying "But I have a mom!" (00:07:24). The fact that this is the case does not show that there is a flaw in his thinking; on the contrary, it shows that even though Zucchini knows what death means, he still finds it emotionally very difficult to come to terms with the idea that his mother is permanently gone.

The industry-versus-inferiority stage, as described by Erikson, involves middle childhood and is a period when children develop a feeling of competence as a result of achieving successes, taking part in social activities and being recognised (Erikson, 1963; Feldman, 2017, p. 387). When Zucchini first enters the house, the circumstances are such as to endanger this emerging sense of self-worth. Simon refers to him as 'potato' and invites him to 'prison' (Barras, 2016, 00:12:23). His social position is then reversed during the incident with the kite: after having resisted Simon he is given the approving chant 'ace Zucchini' (00:15:57–00:16:31). He later gives a confident account of the home to Raymond (00:19:54) and takes part in the plan about Camille (00:50:00). Because of these repeated experiences of mastery he changes from being a passive recipient of events to becoming a child who is capable of effective action.

3. Attachment and Caregiving

Attachment theory explains how early relationships shape expectations about safety and availability. According to Bowlby (1969), attachment can be understood as a lasting bond in which the caregiver acts as a secure base, and Ainsworth and others (1978) showed that it is important for the caregiver to be responsive if secure patterns of behaviour and exploration are to be developed. Feldman (2017, pp. 382–383) reaches a similar conclusion by emphasizing the role of responsive care in promoting security. Concerning his mother, Zucchini shows a psychologically ambivalent attitude. When the scene opens, he goes up to the attic as her angry state, which is caused by drinking, intensifies, including her threatening punishment (Barras, 2016, 00:04:27–00:04:58). The same individual is therefore connected with both affection and fear. The fact that he later describes her as one who drank but also cooked and at times played with him (00:06:46) shows that his attachment cannot be simply put down as either "good" or "bad" parenting.

Les Fontaines offers a different kind of care environment. Raymond deliberately retains the name 'Zucchini' because his mother gave it to him (Barras, 2016, 00:09:18), which establishes a connection between his previous self and his present life. When Rosy agrees to Raymond's request to go home, she separates him from the other children and informs him that his mother is not there anymore, at the same time reassuring him that he will be safe (00:16:47–00:17:20). The way she responds mixes honesty with emotional containment.

Raymond ends up being the main source of his ongoing relationship. When Zucchini worries that Raymond will stop coming to see him, Raymond replies to him, “I come to see you because I like you” (Barras, 2016, 00:21:15–00:21:36). It is important to distinguish between a duty one has towards someone and caring for them as a free choice. Later on, Zucchini takes the initiative in contacting Raymond and tells him about Camille (00:36:59), which shows that Raymond has become a secure base for his relationships. The proposal—“Yours and Camille’s… it would be your home” (00:52:52)—is the end of this process; Bowlby's framework allows us to understand why consistent responsiveness can alter people's expectations as to whether other people will remain available (Bowlby, 1969).

4. Stress, Grief, Coping, and Resilience

Stress is made up of the psychological responses that arise when demands are seen as either threatening or challenging. As Lazarus and Folkman (1984) point out, to a certain degree, the way in which people cope is determined by the way in which they evaluate and handle these demands. Feldman (2017, pp. 414–415, 454–462) also observes that people's responses to grief and stress differ depending on the circumstances, their personality, the stage they are at in their development, and the level of social support they have. That is why Zucchini's reactions should be seen as personal responses to having lost someone rather than simply being taken as indications of a mental disorder.

His sorrow can be seen in the fact that he is unable to sleep (Barras, 2016, 00:15:06), in his yearning for his mother (00:16:47), in his nightmares (00:37:36), and in his symbolic connection with objects from his previous life. On the snow trip, he takes a beer can with him as 'a souvenir from my mom' even though it causes him to be mocked (00:28:47–00:28:54); later on, he keeps her letter and her MP3 player (00:38:12) and recalls the height marks she had made on the doorframe (00:43:16). These objects act as tangible connections to an absent attachment figure.

To deal with stress, Zucchini has two main approaches: one is emotion-focused coping, which allows him to manage his feelings, and the other is problem-focused coping, which involves trying to change the stressful situation (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Feldman, 2017, p. 462). The humorous stories that he tells Raymond (00:19:54) might have an emotion-regulating effect, and his writing — 'Since you left, we don’t laugh much' (00:49:09) — offers a way of expressing grief. In response to Camille's threat to expel him, he acts in a problem-focused way by taking part in the hunger strike (00:48:32) and joining the recording plan (00:50:00).

It is possible to view resilience as the ability to adapt in a positive manner when having to deal with serious difficulties, and Masten (2001) stresses that resilience should not be seen as a form of exceptional invulnerability but rather as the way in which normal adaptive mechanisms operate. This is an important point. Zucchini's resilience is supported by his stable routines, supportive adults, acceptance from his peers, opportunities to feel competent, friendships, and social support (Feldman, 2017, p. 463; Siraji, 2024a). At Christmas, his crying is accompanied by happiness since "sometimes we cry because we're happy" (Barras, 2016, 00:58:49). In this respect, resilience does not mean that Zucchini stops grieving; instead, he is able to keep on forming relationships, finding occasions of happiness, and taking part in ordinary activities even though he is grieving.

5. Peer Relationships and Belonging

During middle childhood, peer relationships become more important as children learn to see things from other people's point of view, engage in social comparison, show empathy, and display prosocial behaviour through their interactions with others (Feldman, 2017, pp. 384–385, 583–585). According to Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological systems theory, development takes place within one's immediate social environments. The peer group therefore acts as a psychological microsystem which can either marginalise or support him.

When Simon joins the group, he is met with hostility since Simon says at (Barras, 2016, 00:12:23), "Welcome to prison, potato!", in effect treating him as an outsider. Following the incident with the kite, though, the group refers to him as "ace Zucchini" (00:16:31). Later on Simon tells the children's backgrounds and Zucchini then reveals his own guilt (00:19:13–00:19:29). The statement Simon makes that "We're all the same" is pessimistic yet at the same time produces a sense of recognition through the experience of shared suffering.

Camille is of particular importance as their relationship is based on mutual vulnerability. At first she looks after herself by being cautious (00:23:31), but later says to Zucchini, "If I hadn't been brought here, I never would have met you" (00:35:02). When she expresses her fear of going back to her aunt and states, "If I have to go back, I'll kill myself", Zucchini replies, "I won't let you go" (00:40:07). This reaction must not be seen as a clinical intervention; rather, it shows emotional solidarity and the value of having a sense of belonging. The way the children reacted together—by going on a hunger strike and deciding to record themselves (from 00:48:32 to 00:50:00)—shows how empathy can lead to prosocial behaviour. The fact that Simon later made amends (from 00:54:17 to 00:55:30) also demonstrates that a feeling of belonging can last even after an argument has taken place.

6. Social Learning, Self-Efficacy, and Agency

As stated in his social learning theory, Bandura maintained that people learn through the processes of observing, interacting, experiencing reinforcement, and interpreting the outcomes (Bandura, 1977). The idea of self-efficacy that he later put forward concerns people's beliefs regarding their ability to plan and carry out the actions needed to achieve their desired outcomes (Bandura, 1997). It is these concepts that illustrate how Zucchini changed from a state of helplessness to one of agency.

The kite battle scene is a great early mastery experience (Barras, 2016, 00:15:57-00:16:31). We see Zucchini discover that resistance can make the difference; a skilful act can help make him look good to others. The kite battle involving Camille provides an experience that is multi-dimensional in terms of mastery—acting skill—and takes on a collaborative character (00:48:32-00:50:00). We witness the models of patience, honesty, and emotional repression (Raymond and Rosy), but by being repeatedly treated in a reliably dependable way, the caring becomes less a response and more something Zucchini can reproduce for Camille.

7. Conclusion and Personal Reflection

The positive development of Zucchini cannot be fully accounted for by any one theory alone. According to Piaget, we can explain the development of the ability to reason logically and causally; Erikson explains the importance of feeling competent and being recognised; Bowlby and Ainsworth demonstrate the psychological significance of having reliable care; Bandura accounts for the development of agency and self-efficacy; Lazarus and Folkman show how people cope; and Masten and Bronfenbrenner show the way in which resilience is maintained through adaptive relationships and environments.

Above all, the film shows that development is relationally determined. Once Zucchini has been suffering, he won't recover; rather, he changes since the world in which he lives becomes predictable and thus new expectations can emerge. Rosy honestly replies that Raymond continues to turn up, and the children eventually get used to him. The transition from "I want to go home to my mom" (Barras, 2016, 00:16:47) to the final atmosphere of belonging proves that it is possible to grieve a lost attachment without this preventing the formation of new attachments.

The film shows that protection for children should be all-embracing and involve psychological safety. They must also feel care and concern, be acknowledged and valued, belong, and be able to feel competent through the opportunity to demonstrate their skills and talents. As Bronfenbrenner stated in 1979, development mainly takes place as a result of engagement and interaction with others, a finding that is in line with the ecological approach. Zucchini shows that difficult behaviors reflected by traumatized children should not be considered indicators of mental disorders; behaviors could be messages from children who are withdrawn and/or scared, whose backgrounds cannot be easily perceived by adults. When children have reliable relationships, repeated hardships should not lead to an unfortunate life.
