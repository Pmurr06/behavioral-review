Emotions as a Teacher: Remembering Rhymes for Young Ghouls

Rhymes for Young Ghouls (Jeff Barnaby, 2014)

James Machado

Philosophy
University of Oregon

Philosophy August 2026 8 min read

Introduction

Jeff Barnaby’s Rhymes for Young Ghouls examines the injustice experienced by Canadian Indigenous people not simply by explaining it through a story, but by forcing the viewer to experience it (Barnaby, 2014). The film deliberately employs the use of color, image quality, sound, perspective, and controls the speed of the film so expertly that it is able to construct a world in which violence is unavoidable, freedom is conditional, and the very individuality of the characters is constantly under threat. Instead of offering some sort of emotional relief or resolution at any point, the film insists on discomfort, forcibly dropping the viewer into the unstable and oppressive reality of life on Canadian reservations during this time. Rhymes for Young Ghouls by Jeff Barnaby seeks to evoke emotions of powerlessness and entrapment by withholding true resolution from the audience in an effort to prevent the viewers from forgetting the experience it forces.

Color and Visual Experience

The repeated use of the color red is one of the most memorable examples of film thinking throughout this film. Blood is everywhere, a constant reality of life on the reservation, a tax that all must pay. It appears after brutal and gory acts of violence, in which characters are continuously beaten, bruised, and battered. It is present from the beginning of the movie, when Tyler is run over by his mother, then when Burner is beaten by his fellow Indians, then when Joe beats up the bullies at St. Dymphna’s when he was younger, and again when Aila and her father are beaten on the beach in the present day. Each of these scenes featured a ridiculous amount of blood, oftentimes disproportionate to the actions that caused the bleeding in the first place. This repetitive violence, coupled with the stark, scarlet color of blood, is a constant reminder of the injustice faced by the Mi’gMaq Indigenous people. Its emphasis through repetition elicits strong feelings of frustration, and helplessness within the viewer. The red is not simply for show, but to draw attention to the fact that injustice carries real consequences. Rhymes for Young Ghouls uses color to prevent the viewer from emotionally distancing oneself from the events of the film. Rather than allowing for the feeling of simply watching a movie, the film forces us to examine how normalized and unavoidable the violence within these systems truly is, and how the characters are forced to live in and adapt to these mechanisms of injustice.

Image quality within the film functions to intensify the feelings of helplessness and frustration. When Aila’s braids are cut as she is admitted into St. Dymphna’s, the image becomes blurry. Here, the film seeks to disallow passive viewership. The viewer is not allowed to simply see the events unfold, but must experience what it felt like to be there, to lose a form of self-expression, to be stripped of individuality, of meaning. The internal confusion and complete powerlessness felt by Aila is emphasized through the lack of visual clarity in the scene. The viewer, in turn, becomes more than just a spectator, but an experiencer. Removed from the bench and dealt onto the field of play, we as viewers must examine not only the events as they unfold, but how they make us feel.

Sound and Perspective

Sound in many films serves as a narrator. It creates suspense, illustrates emotions, and sets the scene. The sound in Rhymes for Young Ghouls does not function as a normal narrator, however. It is unreliable and inconsistent, with music often appearing too early or too late, or lingering longer than expected. This creates a sense of suspense and unease throughout the film that never really goes away. Sometimes it leads absolutely nowhere and nothing happens. Other times, it culminates in brutal violence. Additionally, the sound overwhelms dialogue during certain scenes, functioning to obscure voices and meaning, to confuse. Even moments of calm, with a marked lack of sound, are temporary and provide no relief at all. For example, when Aila reunites with her father after breaking out of St. Dymphna’s, the absence of music allows for Aila to be emotionally vulnerable with her father, to create a connection. We as viewers are compelled to relax, as though the conflict of the film has ended and we are about to witness the resolution. However, this feeling is immediately cut short as the music begins abruptly again and Popper enters the scene. Rather than preparing us for the scene or at least narrating how we should feel, the sound was completely absent. Instead of helping the viewer understand the world created by the film, it is utilized to make us feel unsafe within it.

The film continues to exert its expert control over the audience, most significantly through the way it controls focus and perspective. The story is almost entirely restricted to Aila’s point of view, locking us as viewers into her experience. By emphasizing this, the film in turn emphasizes the limitations under which Aila operates, both as a woman and an Indigenous person. Throughout the film, we watch her solve the problems of others, endure repeated physical abuse, and pay literal costs for her freedom. She takes it upon herself to make up for Sholo getting robbed, and is punched by Milch while innocently riding her bike. These events matter because they are not the only times Aila is subjected to pain from her friends, family, and her foes. The film highlights her disadvantage throughout, being a young woman in a minority group. By confining the perspective of the film strictly to that of Aila, the viewer is made to feel trapped, to feel the way Aila and so many other Canadian Indigenous people under this oppression feel, making it impossible to escape the subject of the film.

Pace, Discomfort, and Entrapment

Although it can be argued that the film’s mixed pace takes away from its immersive effects, the change in speed actually contributes to how the viewer perceives the film’s content, and whether or not they see it as real events with real stakes. An example of this shift in cadence occurs during the scene in which Aila and her father sit together on the boat. There is very little happening, as they reminisce on Aila’s mother and how much she loved being on the water. The film prolongs the stillness a little longer than what feels normal, creating a sense of awkwardness, as though the viewer is intruding on a private moment. Then, it cuts to the next scene, and we are abruptly thrust back into violence. The pause, however short, allows for momentary relief for the audience. That split second within the movie, when there is nothing happening, when there is silence, and peace, is the moment when the viewer can begin to reflect on all of the events that have transpired up to this point, and how they feel about them. However, this film is relentless, creating an experience that doesn’t give the viewer time to breathe. Just as soon as one is allowed some respite, they are thrust right back into the brutal violence that is the reality of the film. By affording the audience a little bit of relief, then forcibly taking it all the same, the film not only preserves its immersive effect but directly mirrors the reality of reservation life in this movie. Just as the audience is not afforded comfort, neither are the characters who suffer this injustice everyday.

Some viewers respond to the film by describing it as a “bad movie,” not because it wasn’t exciting, or because the characters were poorly written, but because it made them feel terrible. They will remember these feelings, likely long after they forget the plot. The film prevents the audience from forgetting these emotions, consequently preventing the audience from contradicting the reality of the characters. The Indigenous people who suffered this kind of inequality could not escape it. In turn, the film seeks to prevent the audience from escaping it too.

Conclusion

Through its unique use of color, image quality, sound, perspective, and speed, Rhymes for Young Ghouls not only tells the story of oppression, it makes the viewer feel its weight. Barnaby’s film forces the viewer to recognize that injustice is not something abstract, or something that is there only some of the time, but something that is lived through everyday. By preventing the viewer from feeling comfortable, from seeing clearly, or from experiencing the relief felt as a result of watching most movies, the film forces the audience to confront the brutal reality of a system designed to break individuality and spirit, creating a film and an experience that will not soon be forgotten. Although the experience is painful, the events of the movie graphic, the death of the characters sad, and the oppression of the Canadian Indigenous people deplorable, this kind of film must exist. It must exist, as it more than just teaches the history of these events. It makes forgetting them impossible.
