Balancing Climate Policy with Economic Interests

The cost of ignoring climate change grows every year, yet some argue that taking action will harm the economy. Can the United States afford to wait? Consider the last time a credit card payment was not made on time. By the time the balance was paid, the total cost had increased significantly due to accumulated interest. The United States is accumulating environmental debt in a similar way, except the long-term costs are growing exponentially.

Although transitioning to renewable energy requires substantial upfront investment, the economic consequences of inaction are far greater. Balancing climate policy with economic interests is essential to minimizing rising disaster relief costs, reducing the hidden costs of fossil fuel production, preventing a significant decline in gross domestic product (GDP), and mitigating the impacts of rising sea levels. These economic challenges continue to intensify as climate-related disasters become more frequent, fossil fuel production imposes increasing environmental costs, global GDP projections worsen, and sea levels continue to rise.

Understanding Climate Change

Climate change has been one of the most widely debated political and environmental issues of the past several decades. Global temperatures have reached unprecedented levels, contributing to more frequent climate-related disasters, rising sea levels caused by melting ice caps, and the degradation of ecosystems such as coral reefs. Recent advancements in monitoring ice loss and sea level rise indicate that climate change continues to accelerate (Harvey, 2024). Although Earth's climate has changed throughout history, the current rate of warming is unlike anything observed during the past 10,000 years (Editorial Board, 2024).

What is driving this unprecedented rate of change? According to NASA, scientific understanding has evolved from viewing human-caused climate change as a theory to recognizing it as an established fact (NASA, 2025). The Earth's atmosphere naturally traps heat through the greenhouse effect, allowing the planet to sustain life. However, human activities—including the burning of coal, oil, and natural gas—have dramatically increased concentrations of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane. Combined with widespread deforestation, these activities have accelerated the environmental changes observed today.

Despite the growing scientific consensus, many policymakers and members of the public argue that transitioning to renewable energy would impose unacceptable economic costs. While the upfront investment required for this transition is substantial, it remains far smaller than the economic costs already being incurred—and those projected to accumulate in the coming decades—as a result of climate change.

The Economic Cost of Climate Disasters

In 2024, the United States experienced unprecedented financial losses from climate-related disasters. The ten costliest events resulted in approximately $229 billion in damages and claimed more than 2,000 lives (Harvey, 2024). In addition, the country experienced a record-breaking 28 weather and climate disasters in 2023, surpassing the previous record of 22 events set in 2020 (Editorial Board, 2024). These statistics demonstrate the growing economic consequences of climate inaction. As climate-related disasters become more frequent and severe, the financial burden on the United States continues to increase.

According to the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), rising sea levels and the increasing frequency of extreme weather events threaten critical infrastructure, placing significant economic strain on insurance systems and government disaster relief efforts (Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [IPCC], n.d.). Looking ahead, the cumulative cost of repairing and replacing damaged infrastructure is expected to rise substantially as climate impacts continue to intensify.

The Hidden Costs of Fossil Fuels

A group of economists from Yale University and the University of Pennsylvania examined the economic impacts of climate change in Climate Change through the Lens of Macroeconomic Modeling. One of the study's most significant findings concerns the hidden environmental cost of fossil fuels. As James Pethokoukis (2024) explains, scientists estimate that carbon emissions impose an environmental cost of approximately $61 per metric ton of carbon dioxide, increasing the true cost of gasoline by roughly $0.53 per gallon. Although these costs are rarely reflected in market prices, they accumulate rapidly given the nation's dependence on fossil fuels.

Beyond their environmental impacts, fossil fuels are becoming less economically competitive than renewable energy. Since 2010, the cost of utility-scale solar energy has declined by 82%, while onshore wind costs have fallen by 39% (International Energy Agency [IEA], 2020). These reductions have been driven by technological innovation and increasingly competitive supply chains. By 2023, approximately three-quarters of newly installed renewable energy projects were more cost-effective than existing fossil fuel facilities (IEA, 2020). Unlike fossil fuels, which are finite resources subject to volatile market prices, renewable energy provides a more stable and cost-effective long-term energy source.

The economic advantages of renewable energy extend beyond lower production costs. As renewable technologies continue to become more affordable, they strengthen the long-term financial case for transitioning away from fossil fuels. The following section examines how climate change is projected to affect global gross domestic product (GDP).

Climate Change and Global GDP

Jenni Doering, an economics reporter for Inside Climate News, discusses research projecting the long-term economic consequences of climate change. According to the article, “Economic modeling projects that proceeding with business as usual without sharply reducing emissions could cut global gross domestic product (GDP) in half as soon as 2070” (Doering, 2025). Such a decline is largely attributed to projections that some regions of the world will become increasingly uninhabitable, disrupting agriculture, infrastructure, and business operations. The resulting decline in GDP is also expected to increase the number of people at risk of hunger, with particularly severe impacts projected for regions such as Africa and West Africa.

These projections build upon the economic costs already discussed. The United States is currently spending billions of dollars responding to climate-related disasters, while economists project that the long-term consequences of continued inaction will be substantially greater. Although the need for stronger climate policies appears increasingly evident, one major obstacle remains: the cost of transitioning to renewable energy. Critics often argue that the required investment is too expensive and unlikely to generate sufficient economic returns. The following section examines the estimated cost of that transition and whether those concerns are supported by current economic research.

The Cost of Transitioning to Renewable Energy

Estimating the total cost of transitioning to renewable energy globally involves numerous factors, including technological advancements, implementation speed, and geographic differences. The International Energy Agency (IEA) and other researchers have developed extensive models to forecast these costs. According to the IEA's World Energy Outlook, achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 would require clean energy investments to more than triple by 2030, reaching approximately $4 trillion (International Energy Agency [IEA], 2020). This estimate includes investments in renewable energy technologies, energy efficiency measures, and the grid infrastructure necessary to support a decentralized power generation system.

Although these investments are substantial, research from Oxford University suggests that accelerating the transition to renewable energy could generate savings of approximately $12 trillion by 2050 (Way et al., 2022). These projected savings account for both the declining costs of renewable technologies and the avoided economic damages associated with climate change. The cost-effectiveness of this transition also depends on the continued decline in renewable energy costs discussed earlier. While the upfront investment is significant, the long-term economic, environmental, and public health benefits justify the transition. Investing in renewable energy not only reduces carbon emissions but also has the potential to stabilize and lower global energy costs over time.

Rising Sea Levels and Future Economic Risks

Rising sea levels pose a significant threat to coastal regions worldwide, exacerbating problems such as coastal erosion, increased flooding, and the loss of habitat for plants, animals, and humans. Florida is a clear example of these challenges. The state has experienced noticeable increases in sea level, which have accelerated in recent decades. According to the Florida Climate Center, sea levels around Virginia Key have risen by approximately 8 inches since 1950, with a noticeable acceleration over the past decade. Projections suggest that by 2070, Florida could lose as much as 3.5 million acres of coastal land. Worldwide, nearly 3 million acres of farmland may also be lost (Florida Climate Center, n.d.). The loss of farmland contributes to declines in global GDP. Considering that these projections only extend over the next 45 years, continuing along the current trajectory will only magnify the economic and environmental consequences in the future.

Policy Recommendations

Considering the economic costs that have been established, the U.S. government must prioritize immediate climate action. Not only is the country already paying the price for past inaction, but current projections also indicate a significant future decline in GDP. Additionally, the United States risks falling behind in the global economy if it continues along its current course. Each day of inaction contributes to further irreversible damage.

There are several initiatives the government could pursue to strengthen both economic stability and climate resilience. Rejoining the Paris Agreement, expanding clean energy incentives, and implementing a carbon tax would help guide the transition toward renewable energy. Rejoining the Paris Agreement is one of the most significant actions the United States could take. The Paris Agreement is an international treaty aimed at limiting global warming, with more than 195 participating countries. It requires nations to establish nationally determined contributions (NDCs) to reduce greenhouse gas emissions.

However, the Trump administration withdrew from the agreement, arguing that it would harm the economy. Ani Dasgupta, President and CEO of the World Resources Institute, stated that this decision “hands China and the European Union a competitive edge in the booming clean energy economy and leads to fewer opportunities for American workers” (Dasgupta, as cited in Moon et al., 2018). China and the European Union continue to make significant investments in renewable energy, electric vehicles, and green technology. The United States stepping away from the agreement has allowed these nations to strengthen their positions within the clean energy market, placing the U.S. at a competitive disadvantage. As the global clean energy economy continues to expand, the United States must strategically position itself to remain competitive.

Expanding Climate Policy

Rejoining the Paris Agreement is only part of the solution. The U.S. must also expand clean energy incentives and establish a federal carbon pricing system. Under this pricing system, the government would impose a tax or fee on businesses' carbon emissions. The goal is to discourage carbon-intensive practices by incentivizing more sustainable alternatives. Taking these steps would not only reduce emissions but also help stimulate the economy.

Critics argue that these policies will harm businesses, but this concern does not align with current economic trends. Although the upfront investment is substantial, it is well worth the cost when compared to the consequences of continued inaction. The clean energy industry is growing much faster than the fossil fuel sector, and solar and wind energy already support millions of jobs in the U.S. Moreover, renewable energy is becoming less expensive than coal and natural gas, meaning it has the potential to reduce electricity costs over time.

Failing to act with urgency will have severe consequences in the coming decades. Continuing along the current path will not only devastate the environment and economy but also harm millions of people, particularly those in vulnerable communities. These policies would restore the United States to a leadership role in global climate action while helping prevent further environmental destruction. Although critics argue that climate action is too expensive, the reality is that the cost of waiting is far greater. Additionally, continued inaction will place the United States at a competitive disadvantage in rapidly growing global industries. The time to act is now.

Conclusion

The economic case for climate action is no longer speculative. The United States is already absorbing the mounting costs of climate-related disasters, rising infrastructure strain, hidden fossil-fuel externalities, and worsening long-term GDP projections. While transitioning to renewable energy requires substantial investment, the evidence presented throughout this article shows that delay is the far more expensive option.

Balancing climate policy with economic interests does not require choosing between environmental responsibility and prosperity. It requires recognizing that long-term economic stability increasingly depends on immediate action. Rejoining international climate efforts, expanding clean energy incentives, and adopting carbon pricing would help reduce future damages while strengthening U.S. competitiveness in the global economy. The longer action is postponed, the more costly the consequences will become.
