The Desktop Clinic: Can the Therapeutic Alliance Survive the Digitalisation of Therapy?

Nya Miller-Davies

Cardiff Metropolitan University

Psychology October 2026 10 min read

Introduction

Envision entering a therapy session without leaving the room you are currently sitting in. No waiting room, no consulting room and perhaps not even a shared physical space between therapist and client, only a screen connecting two people. The digitalisation of psychological therapy has transformed how psychological support can be accessed, offering greater flexibility and potentially reducing geographical, physical and practical barriers to treatment. Nevertheless, concerns over the complexity of creating meaningful connections whilst treating patients become more common with the developing nature of therapy beyond the traditional consulting room. At the centre of this debate is the therapeutic alliance. The collaborative relationship between therapist and client, manifesting agreement on therapeutic goals and tasks alongside the development of such a bond. Its significance is well established. In a meta-analysis of 295 independent studies involving more than 30,000 patients, Flückiger et al., (2018) identified a significant positive association between alliance quality and treatment outcomes, with a comparable association observed in internet-based psychotherapy. However, contemporary research has also highlighted challenges in conceptualising and measuring the alliance across face-to-face and online forms of psychotherapy, particularly as different forms of digital therapy may create different conditions for therapeutic connection (Saxler et al., 2024). This makes the therapeutic alliance particularly relevant to the digitalisation of psychological therapy: if trust, collaboration and interpersonal connection are fundamental to therapy, what happens when these processes are mediated by technology?

There are legitimate reasons to question whether digital therapy can fully reproduce the relational qualities of face-to-face treatment. Physical separation may limit access to subtle non-verbal cues, while technological disruptions can interrupt communication and emotional continuity. Privacy concerns may also affect how openly clients communicate when therapy takes place within the home. However, the assumption that technological mediation necessarily weakens the therapeutic relationship is not consistently supported by empirical evidence. Seuling et al., (2024), found statistically significant differences in therapeutic alliance between videoconferencing and in person psychotherapy. Aafjes-van Doorn et al., (2024) similarly found a significant association between therapeutic alliance and treatment outcomes in teletherapy.

Evidently, there is a more complex situation between online and in-person therapy. Digital therapy encompasses diverse forms of psychological support, from live videoconferencing to asynchronous messaging and self-guided interventions, each of which may shape therapeutic connections differently. Determinants of the compromise of the therapeutic alliance are communication, non-verbal interaction, rapport, anonymity, and accessibility are large contributors to the intricacies of the rise in digital therapy.

What exactly is the Therapeutic Alliance?

The therapeutic alliance is not simply whether the client “likes” their therapist. One of the most influential conceptualisations comes from Bordin’s model, which defines the working alliance through three interrelated components: agreement on therapeutic goals, agreement on the tasks required to achieve those goals, and the development of a bond between therapist and client. This makes the alliance inherently collaborative. It is not simply an emotional connection, but a working relationship through which therapeutic change is pursued. If the alliance depends partly upon collaboration and shared therapeutic goals, physical proximity may not necessarily be essential. A therapist and client can agree on goals, complete therapeutic tasks and develop trust through a digital platform. However, the way in which these processes occur may change considerably.

Saxler et al., (2024) demonstrates this importance through their systemic review which identified 48 instruments used to measure therapeutic alliance in adult psychotherapy, two of which were specifically designed for online psychotherapy. The authors argue that the considerable diversity in how alliance is conceptualised and measured makes coherent research difficult. Indicating that a possibility that established face-to-face conceptualisations may not fully capture the alliance formed through different digital environments.

Such conclusions raise a methodological and psychological problem. Transferring an alliance to a conventional or a digital therapy does not indicate a necessity that it measures the same phenomenon for both cases. When there is no synchronous conversation, the problem becomes even more complex. Asking whether digital therapy has “the same” therapeutic alliance as face-to-face therapy may itself be too simplistic. Before comparing the two, researchers must consider what type of digital therapy is being examined and whether the alliance operates in precisely the same way.

The case against the screen

There are intuitive reasons to suspect that digital therapy could interfere with therapeutic connection. Traditional psychotherapy allows therapists and clients to share a physical environment with communication occurring through speed, facial expression, posture, eye contact and other non-verbal behaviours. A screen inevitably mediates some of this interaction.

Non-verbal communication may be particularly relevant when clients struggle to articulate emotional experiences. A therapist sitting physically opposite a client may notice changes in posture, facial expression, movement or silence that provide additional information about the client’s emotional state. Video conferencing preserves some of these cues, but framing, camera position and screen size can alter what is visible. In text-based therapy, most non-verbal information disappears entirely. Technology can introduce further disruptions. Poor internet connection, audio delays, frozen screens or unexpected disconnections can interrupt conversational rhythm. While individually minor, repeated disruptions could interfere with emotional continuity and make therapeutic interactions feel less natural.

The physical environment also plays a significant role. Face-to-face therapy generally provides a designated therapeutic space, whereas digital therapy can occur in areas like a bedroom, kitchen, university accommodation or shared household. A client may worry about being overheard, particularly when discussing sensitive experiences. They may also be interrupted by family members, pets or other responsibilities. Consequently, the digital environment can introduce privacy and boundary issues that do not necessarily arise within a clinical setting.

There is also the question of psychological distance. A screen may reduce some of the interpersonal intensity associated with sitting directly opposite another person. For some clients, this could make disclosure easier; whilst for others, it may create emotional distance. Importantly, these possibilities cannot be assumed to affect everyone to the same degree.

Indeed, earlier research illustrates why the evidence has historically been mixed. Norwood et al., (2018), found that videoconferencing psychotherapy produced a good working alliance overall, but their meta-analysis did not establish non-inferiority of the alliance compared with face-to-face treatment. Such findings demonstrate that the possibility of an alliance difference cannot simply be dismissed. However, evidence of potential challenges should not be mistaken for evidence that digital therapy inherently produces a poorer therapeutic relationship.

The case for alliance surviving online

More recent evidence provides a considerably more nuanced picture. Seuling et al., (2024) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 publications comparing therapeutic alliance in video conferencing psychotherapy with psychotherapy delivered in person. They found no statistically significant difference between the two formats in alliance ratings provided by either patients or therapists. Patient rated alliance produced a standardised mean difference of –0.09, while therapist rated alliance produced a standardised mean difference of 0.04. This directly challenges the assumption that physical co-presence is a prerequisite for a strong therapeutic alliance. If therapist and client can establish comparable alliance ratings while communicating through video conferencing, then the physical consulting room cannot be considered essential to the relationship in every circumstance.

However, a lack of statistically significant difference should not be interpreted as proof that the two experiences are identical. The meta-analysis examined videoconferencing specifically, rather than multiple platforms of digital therapy. Digital and face-to-face therapy thus cannot be deemed psychologically indistinguishable based on the statistic.

Evidence from other forms of digital therapy further complicates the assumption that therapeutic connection depends upon physical presence. Van Lotringen et al., (2021) conducted a systematic scoping review of 23 studies examining therapeutic alliance in text based digital psychotherapy. Most interventions involved asynchronous communication, such as email or integrated messaging systems. Overall, alliance scores were generally high and most reported relationships between alliance and treatment outcomes were positive. Text-based therapy removes many of the interpersonal cues traditionally considered important to psychotherapy. There is no facial expression, vocal tone or physical presence. Yet a therapeutic relationship can still develop through written communication such as email or integrated messaging systems. Overall, alliance scores were generally high and most reported relationships between alliance and treatment outcomes were positive.

The absence of non-verbal cues may even produce unexpected advantages for some clients. Written communication gives clients additional time to formulate thoughts and feelings before responding. Some individuals may find the reduced immediacy of text-based interaction less intimidating, potentially facilitating disclosure. Consequently, technology does not simply remove relational possibilities. It can also create new ones.

Perhaps the most important weakness in the debate is the tendency to use digital therapy as though it describes a single experience. Consider three hypothetical therapeutic encounters. In the first, a client speaks to their therapist through a live video call. In the second, the client communicates through asynchronous written messages. In the third, the client completes a self-guided cognitive behavioural therapy programme with minimal therapist contact. All three could be described as digital psychological interventions, but the interpersonal relationships involved are fundamentally different.

For whom does digital therapy work?

Accessibility is one of the strongest arguments in favour of digital psychological support. Videoconferencing can allow people who face geographical, physical or practical barriers to access therapy without travelling to a clinic. For some clients, remaining within a familiar environment may reduce anxiety associated with attending an unfamiliar clinical setting. The same feature could nevertheless be disadvantageous for others. A client living in a crowded household may struggle to find privacy. Another may have unreliable internet access or limited technological confidence.

Therapist characteristics are also significant. Digital therapy requires practitioners to communicate effectively through a different medium. A therapist who is attentive to pauses, changes in tone and facial expressions may adapt successfully to video-based work. Therapeutic communication through asynchronous messaging requires different skills altogether.

Digital therapy should therefore not be treated as a single, uniform intervention. Video conferencing, text-based therapy and self-guided interventions create different opportunities for communication and therapeutic interaction. Their effects on the therapeutic alliance may differ in significance. Findings from one digital modality cannot be generalized to another medium given their adverse effects.

The focus on traditional or digital therapy should instead shift towards investigating how alternative forms of technology reshape the conditions under which therapeutic relationships develop. Although digital therapy introduces genuine challenges, around communication, privacy and non-verbal interaction, it does not necessarily prevent the development of a meaningful therapeutic alliance.

Conclusion

Digitalization has shifted the conditions in which therapeutic relationships develop. Introducing challenges around non-verbal communication, privacy and technological disruption. However, current evidence does not suggest that these changes necessarily compromise the therapeutic alliance. Instead, the impact of digital therapy appears to depend on the modality used, the client, the therapist and the context in which therapy takes place.

The therapeutic alliance therefore may not require a shared physical space to remain meaningful. Rather than asking whether technology can replace the consulting room, future research should consider how therapeutic connection can be effectively maintained and adapted within increasingly digital forms of psychological practice.
