Bridging the Gap: Addressing Health Disparities in Punjabi Communities Through Culturally Responsive Health Education

By Jasmine Kaur, Mansa Kaur, and Japji Kaur

Sehat e Punjab

Public Health August 2026 18 min read

Introduction

Health literacy, which can be defined as the ability to find, understand, and utilize health information to make informed decisions, is essential for preventing disease and maintaining overall well-being. However, many Punjabi and South Asian communities experience barriers to health literacy due to language differences, cultural beliefs, limited access to culturally appropriate health education, and underrepresentation in public health outreach. Such barriers can make it difficult for individuals to understand medical advice, seek preventive care, and recognize early symptoms of chronic illnesses.

The purpose of this article is to examine the importance of health literacy within Punjabi communities and to demonstrate how organizations like Sehat e Punjab promote healthier communities through culturally tailored education, outreach, and community engagement. The article highlights the role that nonprofit organizations can play in reducing health disparities and improving access to preventive healthcare.

Health Burden in Punjabi Communities

The global and regional burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) and maternal health challenges disproportionately impacts South Asian populations, particularly within the state of Punjab and across Punjabi diaspora communities globally. In Punjab, state-wide epidemiological surveillance utilizing the World Health Organization STEPS methodology demonstrates a high prevalence of cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors, with hypertension affecting 40.1% of adults and diabetes prevalence standing at 14.3% (Thakur & Nangia, 2022). Population-level screening across ten villages in Punjab using the Government of India’s Community Based Assessment Checklist similarly identified 14.4% of adults aged 30 and older as high-risk for NCDs, with 46.0% of high-risk individuals presenting with active hypertension and 12.0% with diabetes (Jaacks et al., 2022).

Despite this significant disease burden, diagnostic awareness and long-term condition control remain critical points of failure across the continuum of care. In Punjab, only 48.3% of hypertensive individuals are aware of their condition, 30.9% receive medical treatment, and a mere 18.3% achieve blood pressure control (Thakur & Nangia, 2022). Community-level screening reinforces this diagnostic shortfall, noting that only 26.8% of hypertensive villagers had been previously diagnosed prior to systematic outreach (Jaacks et al., 2022). A similar pattern exists for metabolic conditions, as only 34.2% of diabetic adults in Punjab are aware of their condition, 28.2% are on prescribed medication, and just 14.2% maintain controlled blood glucose levels (Thakur & Nangia, 2022; Jaacks et al., 2022).

Beyond cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, breast cancer has emerged as the most common malignancy among women in rural Punjab, where baseline clinical awareness and screening practices remain severely compromised, as evidenced by monthly Breast Self-Examination being practiced by only 8% of rural women prior to structured education (Panda et al., 2026).

Furthermore, in underserved rural regions like South Punjab, high rates of maternal, perinatal, and neonatal mortality persist due to unaddressed delivery complications, including postpartum hemorrhage and eclampsia (Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024).

The etiology of chronic health conditions among Punjabi populations is multifactorial, driven by a combination of behavioral, physiological, and genetic determinants. Clinical studies evaluating Punjabi male cardiac patients demonstrate high rates of physical inactivity, with 84% reporting a sedentary lifestyle, alongside dietary imbalances, combined overweight and obesity rates of 43%, and regular tobacco or alcohol use at 20% and 56%, respectively (Kaur & Bains, 2006). Tobacco use and elevated Body Mass Index correlate directly with elevated serum triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and elevated systolic blood pressure (Kaur & Bains, 2006).

In addition to behavioral factors, multivariate regression models confirm that a family history of diabetes or hypertension significantly increases baseline disease risk while simultaneously increasing the likelihood of an individual seeking screening or receiving a formal diagnosis (Thakur & Nangia, 2022).

Barriers to Health Literacy and Care

Health literacy levels and health-seeking behaviors within Punjabi communities are deeply structured by socioeconomic status, formal education, gender roles, and geographic context.

Higher levels of formal education directly correlate with improved Health Information Seeking Behavior and overall health literacy. For example, Punjabi elders with a bachelor’s degree or higher demonstrate a significantly greater likelihood of using mainstream media and specialized health sources compared to those who did not complete secondary school (Thandi et al., 2024). Conversely, lower education levels correlate with reduced digital access, lower baseline health literacy, and a reduced capacity to evaluate health information (Thandi et al., 2024).

Gender roles and social dynamics also heavily influence healthcare access and information exposure. Women in rural Punjab frequently face social constraints, transport limitations, and reduced household decision-making autonomy, all of which hinder timely medical visits (Panda et al., 2026; Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024). In studies evaluating health-seeking behaviors among Punjabi immigrants, female participants were significantly less likely to access mainstream news or digital social networking platforms for health information compared to males (Thandi et al., 2024).

These gendered and educational barriers are further compounded by geographic isolation. Rural populations in Punjab face systemic infrastructure deficits, including understaffed primary health facilities, transportation barriers, and a scarcity of specialized gynecological and cardiovascular care, leading to high rates of drop-off along non-communicable disease and maternal care cascades (Thakur & Nangia, 2022; Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024).

A primary challenge across Punjabi populations, both in South Asia and across the global diaspora, is navigating cultural taboos, linguistic barriers, and structural health system limitations. Deep-seated social stigma surrounding reproductive, gynecological, and breast health frequently deters women from discussing symptoms or seeking early diagnostic screening until complications reach advanced stages (Panda et al., 2026; Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024).

Furthermore, mainstream health materials are frequently published in English or standard national languages without regional dialect adaptation, isolating elders and rural citizens with limited formal literacy (Thandi et al., 2024; Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024).

Due to unfamiliarity with institutional healthcare or digital tools, many Punjabi elders rely heavily on interpersonal networks, such as friends and family, and word-of-mouth rather than primary care providers or official health portals (Thandi et al., 2024). Over 87% of Punjabi elders do not cross-check health information with healthcare providers, and fewer than 3% evaluate source credentials or domain names, leaving communities highly vulnerable to circulating misinformation through informal channels and messaging platforms such as WhatsApp (Thandi et al., 2024).

A majority of people living with noncommunicable diseases in South Asian countries, especially, have inadequate health literacy (Khatiwada et al., 2022). Studies have shown that individuals with higher levels of health literacy tend to participate in healthier lifestyle practices, including limited smoking and alcohol use, and control of body weight (Khatiwada et al., 2022). This also includes better self-efficacy, knowledge, and management of diseases (Khatiwada et al., 2022).

On the other hand, people who are considered to have lower health literacy experience the opposite outcomes — poor disease management, higher hospitalization rates, and lower medication adherence, resulting in higher mortality (Khatiwada et al., 2022). In connection with differences in language proficiency, education, income, and age, access to health information and healthcare services can become even more limited.

Language barriers remain one of the most significant obstacles to healthcare access for Punjabi and South Asian communities, especially among immigrants. In California, where Sikh immigrants have one of the largest first-generation populations in the state, Punjabi speakers have requested language interpretation services in local hospitals more than any other South Asian group (Gokhale, 2025).

Supporting this is the fact that Punjabi immigrants are less likely to be fluent in English compared to other South Asian language speakers, such as Hindi (Gokhale, 2025). With the economy in Punjab being very agriculturally heavy, Punjabi individuals in California tend to gravitate towards more blue-collar jobs, including taxi driving, truck driving, and farm labor work (Gokhale, 2025). As these jobs are largely contained within Punjabi-speaking communities themselves, requiring minimal English speaking, this only heightens the language barrier. Meanwhile, in the corporate and tech industry, English is the primary medium of communication, requiring consistent fluency that naturally builds language proficiency over time. This, in turn, has worked to exacerbate healthcare disparities for Punjabi communities especially.

Gender roles and traditional family norms further contribute to widening the healthcare gap between Punjabi communities and other cultural groups. Unlike the more individualistic values integrated in American culture, South Asian populations have a more collectivist mindset, focusing on family needs and responsibilities over individual priorities (Vakil et al., 2023). In combination with a patriarchal family system, this leads to reinforcing systematic gender expectations where women are expected to carry out domestic work and men should be the main breadwinner (Vakil et al., 2023).

However, on a healthcare level, some South Asian women will naturally prioritize the health and needs of their entire family over their own, which can delay and minimize medical care (Vakil et al., 2023). Supporting this, research studying gender disparities in daughters of Indian mothers for preventive care found that Punjabi-speaking mothers in Ontario are perceived to be at a disadvantage for child appointments when compared to sons (Pulver et al., 2020). This only works to justify the gender-oriented disparities present in healthcare for Punjabi populations.

For many South Asian immigrants, including Punjabi populations, the high cost of healthcare itself deepens health access barriers. In a survey questioning 460 South Asian individuals living in Australia about their healthcare payment choices, only 20% of participants had private health insurance, meaning the rest relied on the country’s public coverage (Nisar et al., 2025).

Similar financial issues are also present in South Asian communities residing in the United States, contributing to financial toxicity (Garg et al., 2025). Even though South Asian Americans have high household incomes overall in the US, this perception often obscures lower-income Indian immigrants, who have higher poverty rates than the white poor population themself (Garg et al., 2025). For the financially vulnerable South Asian populations, they worry significantly about the cost of healthcare disrupting the spread of wealth for basic life necessities, including food and housing, making it difficult for families to maintain financial stability (Garg et al., 2025). Pre-existing financial struggles therefore correlate with lower insurance rates and difficulty in obtaining prescription medicine when needed among the South Asian population (Garg et al., 2025).

Common Misconceptions and Health Beliefs

Beyond socioeconomic factors and systemic constraints in Punjabi communities, common misconceptions remain one of the most significant but overlooked barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment. From cancer screening to putting off routine healthcare checkups, false assumptions and deep-rooted beliefs oftentimes shape health outcomes in Punjabi and other South Asian populations.

While breast cancer remains a leading cause of death among Punjabi women, taking proactive measures to reduce the risk remains significantly low. In a quantitative study focused on identifying breast cancer screening barriers for women in Malwa, Punjab, researchers found that the fatalistic belief that disease is determined by God (Waheguru) was reported as a screening barrier for 81.5% of the participants (Kaur et al., 2023). Since a majority of the women studied were illiterate and considered housewives, it is evident that this misconception is largely a result of limited access to formal education, especially in rural areas of Punjab where ties to healthcare resources are even more constrained.

Without exposure to formal health education, religious ideals like Hukam, which is the Sikh principle of accepting the divine, are more likely to be integrated into everyday decisions. Though Sikhism, the most commonly practiced religion by Punjabis, does not discourage medical intervention, interpretations of the Sikh faith through Hukam appear to be integrated into medical care in a way that was not originally intended. This same pattern likely extends beyond breast cancer, influencing how Punjabi and other Indian communities approach and react to cancer diagnoses and treatment in general.

Mental health stigma in Punjabi communities follows this similar sense of avoidance but is more focused on the fear of social judgment — a fear significant enough to keep people from turning to help even when they need it. A national survey of 1,000 UK Sikhs found that even though 65.7% of people experienced a mental health difficulty, only 33.9% sought professional medical assistance (Uppal et al., 2025).

Much of this worry is correlated with societal status. Unlike in individualistic Western cultures, there is this constant pressure in South Asian households that mental health struggles can hinder a family’s reputation and prospects for arranged marriage, causing significant embarrassment (Uppal et al., 2025). Underlying this shame is the belief that having a mental illness, whether that be depression, anxiety, or others, is genetically inherited — meaning that one family member’s diagnosis is perceived as something that the entire bloodline carries as well (Uppal et al., 2025).

Interestingly, this ideal seems to be passed down through generations as Sikh individuals claim to hear about the genetic predisposition to mental health illness from people around them (Kular et al., 2025).

Additionally, many Indian cultures view mental illness as a Western construct altogether that is not associated with how their own communities traditionally understand psychological suffering. As a result, like other South Asian groups, Punjabis tend to lean towards more religious or spiritual explanations of mental health, as there are no formal definitions for terms like “mental well-being” or “mental distress” (Uppal et al., 2025).

A particular study in the UK examining psychosis within the Sikh community discovered that without proper knowledge of health care vocabulary, Punjabi people rely on adjectives like “pagal,” meaning “mad” in English and often associated with a negative connotation (Kular et al., 2025). This pronounced language barrier has resulted in the lack of translation from more Western-associated illnesses to Punjabi, causing psychosis to go undiagnosed or fall under one blurred category.

Diabetes represents a further health issue where misconceptions lie in Punjabi communities. Due to the amount of Asian immigration into the United States, diabetes rates have been higher than ever in South Asian groups, being 4.88 times more likely to have diabetes compared to the white population (Islam et al., 2014).

According to an interventional study that attended to improving Sikh diabetes health in New York City, many Sikh community members felt no need to take preventive health measures when they had no apparent signs of the disease (Islam et al., 2014). Participants also believed that unlike other medical conditions, diabetes happens to everyone as they age (Islam et al., 2014).

In general, Punjabi and additional South Asian groups tend to view diabetes as a “natural” and inevitable part of life rather than a manageable disease, having a dismissive attitude towards early intervention. This motive reflects the same fatalistic mindset present in the context of cancer, where health issues are oftentimes considered predetermined, reducing the motivation to seek preventative care (Rahim et al., 2024).

Evidence-Based Solutions

Peer-reviewed evaluations demonstrate that targeted, culturally tailored community interventions can successfully overcome these structural barriers and generate measurable improvements in health literacy and health-seeking behavior.

Mobile health interventions have proven effective for broad population-level outreach; for instance, a short message service campaign in urban and rural Punjab delivering 28 automated heart-health text messages over six months achieved knowledge retention rates ranging from 43% to 94% across key cardiovascular topics (Mohan et al., 2017).

Similarly, structured community-based literacy workshops have shown strong results in empowering marginalized groups. A single-session, culturally adapted breast health education workshop for rural women in Punjab resulted in average knowledge scores doubling from 9.82 to 18.45, while monthly Breast Self-Examination practice increased from 8% to 40% and spurred the formation of local peer support groups (Panda et al., 2026).

At the institutional level, integrating frontline healthcare workers into community outreach has generated substantial public health impacts. The “Mothers Matter” initiative in South Punjab trained 280 Lady Health Workers in local dialects to provide targeted counseling on pregnancy danger signs and birth preparedness, reaching over 70,000 caregivers in community sessions and saving 120 maternal lives from postpartum hemorrhage within a twelve-month period (Improving maternal health literacy in South Punjab, 2024).

Synthesis of this collective body of research demonstrates that while systemic health disparities and low baseline health literacy persist across Punjabi communities, interventions that leverage trusted local figures, native language materials, and accessible communication channels effectively bridge the gap between health awareness and preventive action.

Health literacy continues to be an important element in deciding whether people will be able to gain access to health information and comprehend it. There are a number of interventions that can increase health literacy (CDC, 2024).

Community health education is one method that gives people knowledge of ways to prevent diseases, healthcare resources, and good health practices. Community health programs are particularly helpful in that they deliver educational programs directly to the individuals within the community and not having to go through complicated healthcare systems by themselves (CDC, 2024). Such programs make it easier for the individual to understand health and know how to practice good health.

Other key ways in which people may be engaged in their own healthcare decisions include the creation of educational resources in multiple languages and accessible formats. Communication problems due to language barriers might impede people's ability to understand the recommendations regarding their healthcare. Multilingual and clear information helps more people become involved in decisions about their healthcare (CDC, 2024).

Community Health Workers have also been shown to improve health literacy by serving as trusted connections between healthcare systems and communities. Because CHWs often share cultural experiences and backgrounds with the populations they serve, they can explain medical information in ways that are easier to understand while helping individuals access healthcare services (CDC, 2024). Research has demonstrated that CHWs can improve preventive care participation, increase health knowledge, and support individuals managing chronic conditions.

Finally, culturally tailored education plays an important role in addressing health literacy challenges. Health information is more effective when it reflects the cultural values, experiences, and concerns of the community receiving it (CDC, 2024). Programs that incorporate cultural understanding are more likely to build trust and encourage participation in healthcare. Together, these strategies demonstrate that improving health literacy requires accessible communication, trusted educators, and education that reflects the needs of diverse communities.

Translating Research Into Practice: Sehat e Punjab

Sehat e Punjab has sought to translate the health literacy challenges identified in existing research into direct community-based education and engagement. During a recent health fair, attendees were invited to anonymously identify health-related misconceptions they had encountered or previously believed. This activity provided an informal qualitative assessment of the types of misinformation and health beliefs present within the community and allowed students to connect published evidence with the concerns expressed directly by community members.

The responses reflected several of the barriers identified in the literature, particularly misconceptions surrounding chronic disease, preventive healthcare, mental health, and culturally influenced health practices.

Attendees submitted statements including, “Sugar is the only cause of diabetes,” “Eating ghee is very good in limit,” and “If I feel healthy, I don't need a checkup.”

Other responses reflected misconceptions regarding preventive care, including, “You don't need a flu shot if you rarely get sick,” and “Joint pain is just a normal part of getting older.”

Participants also identified beliefs related to sun protection and mental health, including, “Don't use sunscreen if you can just use oil,” “Mental health doesn't matter and isn't that serious,” and “Depression is fake.”

These responses demonstrate how health misinformation can extend beyond a lack of knowledge and become embedded within everyday understandings of health and illness. Several of the misconceptions directly correspond with concerns identified in the literature.

For example, the belief that diabetes is simply caused by consuming sugar and that diabetes is an inevitable condition associated with aging reflects the misconceptions surrounding diabetes prevention and management described among South Asian communities. Similarly, the belief that individuals who feel healthy do not require routine checkups reflects a broader challenge to preventive healthcare, particularly for conditions that may remain asymptomatic until complications develop.

The mental health responses also reinforce previously documented concerns surrounding stigma and the dismissal of psychological illness within Punjabi and Sikh communities.

The activity was conducted among attendees at a Sehat e Punjab health fair and was not designed as a formal epidemiological survey. Nevertheless, the findings provide practical evidence that the barriers and misconceptions described in existing research can manifest within real-world community settings. This illustrates how culturally responsive organizations can use research findings alongside direct community feedback to develop more relevant health literacy interventions.

Future Directions

Despite improvements in the level of health literacy, there is still a lot to achieve. Individuals face barriers such as a lack of health information, language barriers, mistrust in healthcare organizations, and inaccessible educational materials (CDC, 2024).

Further research needs to focus on which health literacy measures are effective for different populations and the ways to scale up successful health literacy interventions while being culturally appropriate. In this case, healthcare organizations need to take health literacy more seriously through the use of plain language, language assistance tools, and health education strategies that will motivate people to ask more questions about their health (CDC, 2024).

Additionally, policymakers should keep funding health education programs, language assistance programs, and projects aimed at increasing access to credible health information for underprivileged populations (CDC, 2024).

Community organizations will continue to play an important role in the development of health literacy since they are usually seen as trustworthy sources of information in the communities they work with. Instead of asking people to find health information on their own, community organizations can provide health education right where they live, study, or pray through outreach activities and educational campaigns.

Conclusion

Health disparities within Punjabi communities are shaped by a complex interaction of chronic disease burden, socioeconomic inequities, cultural beliefs, language barriers, and limited access to culturally responsive health information.

The literature consistently demonstrates that while cardiovascular disease, diabetes, maternal health complications, and certain cancers remain highly prevalent, many individuals are unaware of their conditions or face significant barriers to seeking preventive care and managing chronic illness. These findings show that improving health outcomes requires strengthening health literacy in ways that are accessible and culturally relevant.

Moving forward, continued investment in culturally responsive public health programming, multilingual educational materials, and rigorous evaluation of community-based interventions will be essential.

Strengthening health literacy should be recognized not only as an educational objective but also as a critical strategy for advancing health equity. By empowering individuals with the knowledge and resources needed to make informed health decisions, Punjabi communities can achieve improved health outcomes and build a stronger foundation for long-term community well-being.

About Sehat e Punjab

Sehat e Punjab is a student-run organization dedicated to expanding health awareness and improving access to health education within Punjabi communities. Through community health fairs, educational outreach, and events reaching audiences both locally and internationally, the organization works to make important health information more accessible, culturally relevant, and easier to understand. Sehat e Punjab also engages with community leaders and policymakers to better understand the challenges facing Punjabi families. Most recently, members met with Senator Tim Kearney and Sarah McCullough to discuss community needs and opportunities to better serve Punjabi populations through health education, outreach, and advocacy.
