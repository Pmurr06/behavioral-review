Climate Justice after Institutional Failure

James Machado

Philosophy
University of Oregon

Philosophy September 2026 12 min read

Responsibility Beyond the Individual

Who is the most responsible for a crime? Is the victim of a homicide partially responsible for the situation they put themselves in, or is the perpetrator the most responsible party? Morally driven audiences call for the punishment of the killer, not the killed. If responsibility falls on the perpetrator, why is it that we place the blame for environmental destruction not on the corporations and nations that pollute the most, but on the everyday individual?

We call for reductions in time spent in the shower, more biking to work instead of driving, less use of single-use plastics, and greater reliance on renewable and reusable goods. Society focuses on the macro-scale issue, assuming that humanity acts in unison to harm the planet. These abstract ideals contribute to the responsible parties remaining unchecked for blame. This perspective transforms political and historical injustice into a vague failure attributed to the human species.

Chris J. Cuomo provides insight into this problem in her essay “Climate Change, Vulnerability, and Responsibility,” where she argues that this framework is ethically evasive. Kyle Whyte extends this critique in “Settler Colonialism, Ecology, and Environmental Injustice,” arguing that widely accepted individualistic climate solutions become even more evasive when they ignore settler colonialism and the communities whose collective continuance has been deliberately disrupted by institutions with greater responsibility for preserving the planet.

Responsibility, Power, and Climate Injustice

Cuomo identifies a serious problem with the popular vision of climate responsibility that blames “humans simpliciter,” highlighting the misattribution of equal responsibility for climate change when emissions are disproportionately produced by wealthy nations and large corporations (Cuomo, 2011, p. 697).

She also argues that although household-level solutions are important, they are insufficient on their own. For Cuomo, individual action becomes meaningful when it helps generate collective action capable of producing broader change. The contemporary popular response to climate change can otherwise create disempowerment.

As Cuomo explains, “If … (one) perceives high threat and believes they have low coping ability, they will…[try] to lessen or tolerate fear, anxiety, and helplessness by emotional means such as avoidance, denial, wishful thinking, religious faith, fatalism, and normalization/desensitization” (Cuomo, 2011, p. 703).

When responsibility for the fate of the climate is placed primarily on individuals’ private habits while corporations and states continue business as usual, guilt can prevent meaningful action. Cuomo’s work therefore raises a question not of whether action is necessary, but whether that action produces accountability from those most responsible for perpetuating the ecological crisis.

Settler Colonialism and Collective Continuance

Whyte supplements this argument with a historical and colonial dimension. Settler colonialism is not simply an injustice lost to history, but a form of ecological domination whose effects continue into the present.

Indigenous peoples’ relationships with the lands they inhabit, the water they depend upon, and the systems and traditions that have governed their lives have repeatedly been disrupted. Forced displacement onto unfamiliar or less favorable lands can render strategies and traditions developed over generations less viable, reducing communities’ ability to adapt to a changing climate.

Whyte’s concept of collective continuance “refers to a society’s capacity to self-determine how to adapt to change in ways that avoid reasonably preventable harms” (Whyte, 2018, p. 131).

For Whyte, climate injustice is therefore not simply environmental damage. It is damage that disproportionately affects marginalized Indigenous communities whose capacity to respond has already been constrained by settler colonialism and forces outside of their control.

When Institutions Responsible for Harm Fail

Here, however, another problem emerges. Cuomo is right to assign responsibility to those in power, and Whyte is right to insist that Indigenous communities require continuity with the environments in which they have historically lived. Yet these approaches can still imply some reliance on the institutions most responsible for environmental harm to sufficiently change course before the consequences become irreversible.

That hope is attractive because the alternative is severe, but the political barriers to institutional transformation are substantial. Fossil-fuel infrastructure remains deeply embedded in modern society. Only fifty-seven producers were linked to 80% of fossil CO2 emissions since 2016 (Abnett & Alkoussa, 2024), and there is documented evidence of major corporate networks cultivating doubt and delaying meaningful action on the climate crisis (Geere, 2013).

Attempts to make major polluters financially responsible have themselves encountered institutional resistance. New York, for example, enacted legislation requiring fossil-fuel companies to contribute billions toward climate-related costs (Allen, 2024).

The problem, then, is not simply that justice has not yet been achieved. It is that the structures through which justice is expected to occur can themselves obstruct it.

Preserving the Conditions for Climate Justice

In order to live ethically within contemporary climate constraints, a supplemental approach is necessary. It must bridge the gap between knowing what the planet requires and recognizing what existing political institutions may realistically deliver.

The first priority within such an approach must be preserving and empowering the agency of Indigenous communities to resist further exploitation and ecological damage. Collective continuance must be made possible to a realistic extent.

This does not completely solve the climate crisis, nor is it a form of surrender or resignation. It is a way of protecting the conditions through which future justice remains possible.

If fossil-fuel companies can transition toward cleaner methods or emissions can eventually be substantially reduced, the social, cultural, and ecological foundations necessary for broader reform must still exist when that opportunity arrives.

Combining Cuomo’s critique of individualized responsibility with Whyte’s defense of collective continuance therefore points toward strengthening communities so that they can determine their own futures rather than having their fates sealed by forces outside of their control.

Indigenous-Led Models of Resilience

Contemporary Indigenous-led projects demonstrate the feasibility of this approach.

On the Klamath River, decades of tribal organizing helped remove four dams in the largest dam-removal project in U.S. history, reopening more than 400 miles of salmon habitat. Within weeks, salmon were documented returning to waters that had previously been inaccessible because of the dams. River temperatures also dropped by eight degrees compared with the same month in the previous year (Golden, 2024).

In British Columbia, Indigenous guardian programs patrol more than seven thousand square miles of territory and, in some cases, hold ranger powers to act against poaching and illegal logging (Thomson, 2024). These projects reclaim Indigenous authority while simultaneously promoting environmental recovery and limiting further ecological damage to Indigenous communities.

The same principle can be applied to energy and food dependence.

Native Renewables, an Indigenous, women-led nonprofit organization based in the American Southwest, installs off-grid solar systems with battery storage for Hopi and Diné families while pairing this work with education and workforce training (Native Renewables, 2024). Reducing dependence on centralized fossil-fuel systems can provide communities with greater autonomy.

Similarly, the Swinomish built the first traditional clam garden in the United States in approximately 200 years at Kiket Island, reviving traditional Indigenous food production (“Swinomish Indian Tribal Community,” 2026).

Solar infrastructure and clam gardens are models that can operate outside some of the systems that created the need for them in the first place.

Accountability Without Dependence

Refusing dependence on systems that perpetuate ecological harm should not replace the larger political action necessary to create change at the scale demanded by the climate crisis.

Payments made by major contributors to pollution should not disappear into obscure state budgets or vague, non-actionable initiatives. Funds can instead support Indigenous communities in habitat restoration, land reclamation, and, critically, the transmission of traditional Indigenous knowledge through education.

Recent climate legislation also demonstrates that governments can attempt to hold corporations financially accountable for large-scale pollution. In New York, legislation has sought to impose major costs on fossil-fuel companies associated with climate damage (Allen, 2024).

Free, prior, and informed consent frameworks provide another mechanism for strengthening Indigenous political authority. Such frameworks recognize Indigenous peoples’ rights to self-determination and self-governance in decision-making processes involving projects that affect their lives and resources (“Free, Prior and Informed Consent,” 2026).

The point is not that current systems will suddenly become just. Justice becomes more attainable when reduced dependence is coupled with the restoration of control to communities most vulnerable to ecological harm.

Is This Approach Too Pessimistic?

The pessimistic nature of this argument presents an obvious concern. If institutional reform remains possible, abandoning attempts to pursue it would amount to giving up too early.

Yet the greater surrender may be the repetition of solutions that have historically produced insufficient progress while marginalized communities continue bearing the consequences. This argument does not reject institutional change. Instead, it proposes reducing dependence on institutions that have repeatedly failed to act while continuing to demand change from them.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, approaching climate justice at a smaller scale may raise questions about efficacy. However, small scale does not mean inconsequential.

The preservation and reclamation of Indigenous land, a guardian program, or an off-grid solar network can protect communities, sustain knowledge, and preserve ecological capacity long enough for structural change to become possible.

As Cuomo’s critique of individualized action demonstrates, these changes are not independently sufficient. Their role is to equip communities with the resources and autonomy necessary to continue confronting the climate crisis under better conditions.

Climate Justice after Institutional Failure

Cuomo demonstrates how climate solutions fail when responsibility is diffused across the many in ways that shield the relatively few actors with systemic power from accountability.

Whyte demonstrates how climate justice fails when it ignores the colonial destruction that has shaped Indigenous communities for generations.

The final step is recognizing that, even when institutional repair may be unattainable in the short term, the systems, relationships, resources, and knowledge necessary to make future change possible must still be preserved.

In a non-ideal world, climate justice may therefore require maintaining and expanding present-day Indigenous collective continuance.

Communities most damaged by settler colonialism should not be tasked with simply waiting for the institutions that harmed them to change. They should have access to the resources necessary to govern themselves, restore their communities, and continue living within traditions that have historically sustained their resilience.

This is not a concession of climate justice.

Under conditions of institutional failure, climate justice demands a new approach.
