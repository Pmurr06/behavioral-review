Diary of a Century: Jacques-Henri Lartigue and the Scope of Vernacular Photography

Georgia Lewis

Art History Student
School of the Art Institute of Chicago

Arts & Humanities August 2026 11 min read

Introduction

Vernacular photography is taken by ordinary individuals, typically for personal or practical reasons rather than artistic or professional purposes. Examples include snapshots and family photo albums. Ordinary people often make these photographs hoping to capture memories of family or activities. Photography with a vernacular purpose serves as a memory aid for individuals and families, preserving both important moments and routine activities in a permanent form. Vernacular photography began with the growing popularity of compact cameras introduced by Kodak in the late 1880s.

Vernacular photography is an interesting genre to examine because of its retrospective value as a representation of a specific time and person’s everyday life. With the increasing popularity and accessibility of compact cameras, vernacular perspectives from middle-class individuals became more prevalent. Still, extraordinary exceptions to these average perspectives are essential to understanding history.

Jacques-Henri Lartigue was born in 1894 in France to Henri and Marie Lartigue. His father, Henri Lartigue, was a wealthy banker. At age seven, in 1901, his father gave him his first camera, a large plate camera with a tripod. The camera was so large and elaborate that he needed to stand on a stool to operate it. Lartigue’s perspective on vernacularity is compelling because of his affluence and age. At a time when photographs were often stiff and emotionless, his photography offered something curious and innovative: a fresh, playful view of the world. Lartigue wrote in his diary, “I will be able to photograph everything—everything! And I know very well that many things will ask me to take their pictures, and I will take them.”

During Lartigue’s childhood, France was at the peak of economic prosperity and comfort. Lartigue’s father was a self-made businessman who wanted to provide his family with leisure and luxury. Lartigue secretly thought his father was God in disguise and often prayed to Jesus that he would never grow up. This prosperity supported numerous technological advances, including the automobile and airplane, which became central subjects in his photographs. Lartigue’s photography was largely uninfluenced by dominant aesthetic trends, making his perspective uniquely original despite his relatively mundane subjects. His images portray his age through family, friends, and childlike fascination, illustrating comedy, human absurdity, life, and play.

Later in life, Lartigue moved away from photography and pursued painting. He married and remained a successful but unremarkable artist until his photographs were rediscovered. In 1963, at age 69, Charles Rado of the Rapho agency introduced Lartigue to John Szarkowski, curator at the Museum of Modern Art in New York. Szarkowski then organized an exhibition of Lartigue’s work. Because of its capsule-like quality and distinctive perspective on class and age, his work gained immediate attention after debuting and appeared in Life magazine in late 1963. He published his first photobook in 1967, Diary of a Century. Like most vernacular photographers, Lartigue treated his photographs as memory aids, never intended for public display. The beauty of his work lies in that quality: genuine curiosity, unconcerned with trends or influence.

Richard Avedon called him “the most deceptively simple and penetrating photographer in the history of the art, a gifted observer who has shown us leisure as an adventure and as an indulgence and made us know the full impact of what is lost.” Lartigue’s photographs in Diary of a Century offer a powerful study of vernacularity through three core qualities: childlike genuineness, economic standing and social class, and fascination with the technological advances of his era.

Child-like Qualities

Lartigue in the bath with his hydroglider model (shutter release by his mother, Marie Lartigue), 1904 (Figure One), illustrates Lartigue’s innocence and curiosity. The blurry black-and-white photograph shows Lartigue, age ten, seated in a bathtub with water up to his chin. He smiles spontaneously while his hydroglider toy floats beside him. In the background, frilled curtains line the right side of the image and the corner of a sink appears at top left. The photograph is taken at water level, likely from his mother’s perspective. In “The Ancient Little Boy,” L. Nilson writes that Lartigue “represents photography at its purest, most private, and least self-conscious: They are the product of a brand new medium in the hands of a wide-eyed babe.”

The qualities of childhood are curiosity, creativity, and optimism. Lartigue absorbed the world around him and captured it on film. In 1921, he wrote, “Happiness is a hard thing to explain. My happiness, especially since it still so often resembles what makes a baby smile suddenly in its carriage, without the adults understanding why. The little joys, the large joys—I notice them, I look hard at them, I try to understand them all, always, without stopping.”

Children are among the most curious age groups because the world is still unfamiliar. Lartigue had the unusual opportunity to preserve that curiosity through photography.

Affluence and Influence

Jacques-Henri Lartigue’s father was an affluent and influential banker in France, which placed the family among the upper French bourgeoisie. The haute bourgeoisie included wealthy industrialists, lawyers, bankers, and politicians who gained influence through commerce rather than noble birth. The Lartigue family’s wealth gave Jacques-Henri access to opportunities unavailable to lower economic classes, including automobile races, social parades, and camera ownership, all then closely tied to privilege. Belle Époque high society was defined by exclusivity and spectacle, and Lartigue became one of the few photographers to capture candid moments of everyday life within it.

In 1911, Lartigue took Avenue du Bois de Boulogne, Paris, January 15, 1911 (Figure Two). The image captures a woman walking with two dogs on leash. She holds a direct, intense gaze toward the camera while her face is partially concealed by a lace veil attached to a massive feathered hat. A long fur coat covers most of her body, and horse-drawn carriages pass in both directions behind her. The setting is the avenue bordering Bois de Boulogne, a park known for social display. At the time, Lartigue was seventeen and nearing the end of his early photographic period.

Lartigue was fascinated by what he called the social parade at Bois de Boulogne, where motorcars and open horse-drawn carriages circulated each afternoon. The park’s social life included fairs, charity runs, promenades, and leisurely gatherings in spaces such as Parc de Bagatelle and Jardin des Serres d’Auteuil. In Diary of a Century, Lartigue wrote, “I suppose I go to the park to photograph the ladies in their most outrageous and beautiful hats.” He later described his process: “She approaches ... I’m shy, trembling a little ... 20 metres, ten metres, eight, six ... Clack! My camera shutter makes so much noise that the lady almost jumps as much as I do.”

Cultural Case Study of Technological Advancements

The first automobile was developed in 1886 by Carl Benz, and the first airplane in 1903 by Wilbur and Orville Wright. Photography itself was also a technological marvel of the age. Public fascination with speed and motion shaped modern visual culture, and Lartigue stood at the center of that fascination. From a young age, he became obsessed with movement, encouraged by his father’s gifts and his brother Zissou’s enthusiasm for new machines.

In 1905, at age eleven, Lartigue photographed his cousin leaping a staircase in My Cousin, Bichonnade, Paris, 1905 (Figure Three). Taken from below, the image makes her jump feel monumental. Her dress lifts in motion while she smiles directly at the camera, producing a vivid sense of speed and delight.

In 1904, only a year after the Wright brothers’ first flight, Lartigue and Zissou traveled to witness Gabriel Voisin fly 25 meters. My Brother, Zissou, Gets His Glider Airborne, Château de Rouzat, 1910 (Figure Four) captures the instant Zissou hauls the glider upward. Zissou, committed to aviation mechanics, built flying machines with family and household staff who joined the experiment. Jacques used photography to preserve these moments of invention and play.

In 1912, at age eighteen, Lartigue covered the Gordon Bennett Cup in Metz, one of the era’s major tests of automotive speed. In Grand Prix of the ACF, Dieppe (Figure Five), only part of the race car enters the frame as it tears past. The driver and passenger are the clearest elements while wheels, spectators, and background stretch into blur. The image conveys speed through distortion, showing both technical daring and Lartigue’s instinct for motion.

Significance

Lartigue gave up photography to study painting at the Académie Julian in Paris from 1915 to 1916. He told Life magazine, “Truthfully, I am neither a painter nor a writer nor a photographer. I am simply an ancient little boy, running after the ghost of his game of terrestrial paradise.” Until his rediscovery in 1963, he considered himself a painter first. Because his photographs remained mostly private for decades, they gained the time-capsule quality reflected in the title Diary of a Century.

Between 1963 and 1970, Lartigue’s reputation grew steadily. In 1963, Szarkowski’s exhibition at MoMA introduced him to a wide audience. That same year, Life published his work in “The World Leaps Into an Age of Innovation.” In 1967, he took his first fashion photograph for Harper’s Bazaar in a spread pairing Mary Lancray (1912, Bois de Boulogne) with model Isa Stoppi (1966, Central Park). Richard Avedon later elevated Lartigue among major twentieth-century photographers in relation to Diary of a Century, writing that seeing the photographs was like reading Proust for the first time.

Lartigue’s attention to motion and life secured his importance across fine art, photojournalism, and documentary photography. Filmmaker Wes Anderson has repeatedly cited him as an influence. While preparing Rushmore (1998), Anderson said, “We looked at some pictures by photographer Jacques-Henri Lartigue, who reminds me of Max, and that became a part of it.” At least four Lartigue photographs appear in the film.

At the French premiere of The Life Aquatic with Steve Zissou (2005), Anderson explained that he named the character in homage to Lartigue’s brother Zissou, frequently depicted as a daring adventurer in the photographs. Lartigue is credited in both films.

Lartigue’s work has also shaped everyday photographic practice. In 2020, Daisy Hoppens wrote that her father, gallerist Michael Hoppen, called Lartigue his “Desert Island Photographer” because his images always produce joy. During the COVID-19 pandemic, she drew inspiration from Belle Époque images of promenades, style, and leisure, describing how dressing to walk in a park echoed Lartigue’s own delight in observing people in public spaces.

In street photography, Lartigue’s posthumous influence encouraged artists to become more curious, spontaneous, and open to chance. His photographs model a practice less concerned with predetermined rules and more concerned with attentive observation. Avedon wrote in the afterword to Diary of a Century that Lartigue showed “leisure as an adventure and as an indulgence” while revealing what modern life has lost. Lartigue himself wrote in 1921, “The little joys, the large joys—I notice them, I look hard at them, I try to understand them all, always, without stopping.” Across more than 100,000 photographs, his boyhood images in Diary of a Century remain central to his influence on vernacular and street photography.
