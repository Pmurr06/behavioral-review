Stress and Student Performance in Higher Education

Jacob Gannon

Business & Finance
Washington State University

Psychology August 2026 13 min read

Introduction

Stress is a persistent feature of higher education, but sustained or intense stress can interfere with students’ ability to perform academically. College students often balance heavy course loads, high-stakes examinations, financial pressure, part-time employment, and uncertainty about their futures. These pressures may extend beyond isolated periods such as midterms or finals. In a pan-Canadian study of more than 10,000 post-secondary students, Linden et al. (2022) found that stress and psychological distress remained elevated across multiple points in the academic year. The finding suggests that, for many students, stress is not limited to a brief period that simply ends when an examination or semester is over.

The COVID-19 pandemic offered an especially clear example of how academic and nonacademic stressors can overlap. Mushquash and Grassia (2021) found that students reporting greater pandemic-related stress also reported more depressive symptoms. Zurlo et al. (2020) similarly identified academic disruption, concerns about performance, and social isolation as distinct sources of student stress during pandemic lockdowns. Although pandemic conditions were unusual, these studies demonstrate how pressures surrounding school can interact with broader social and psychological conditions rather than remaining confined to the classroom.

This matters because academic performance can be affected by the conditions under which knowledge is measured. Heissel et al. (2021) examined physiological stress responses during high-stakes testing and found that elevated cortisol responses were associated with meaningful differences in test performance even after accounting for prior achievement. Their findings raise an important question for higher education: when a student performs poorly under acute stress, how accurately does a single high-stakes assessment represent what that student has learned?

The answer is not to eliminate rigor or lower academic standards. Instead, universities should consider how assessment design, coping resources, and institutional support can reduce avoidable barriers to demonstrating knowledge. The research reviewed here suggests that stress is both an individual experience and an institutional concern. Effective responses therefore need to address how students manage stress as well as the academic and material conditions that contribute to it.

Chronic Stress in Higher Education

Evidence suggests that student stress can remain persistent across the academic year. Linden et al. (2022) tracked more than 10,000 post-secondary students in Canada and found consistently elevated stress and psychological distress across measurement points. These findings challenge the assumption that students can simply “power through” periods of academic pressure because the pressures themselves may recur or remain present throughout the year.

The sources of chronic stress are varied. Aloka (2024) identifies academic workload, financial stress, uncertainty about career options, and social isolation among the recurring stressors discussed across higher-education settings. These pressures can overlap. A student concerned about tuition may also be working long hours, carrying a full course load, and worrying about future employment. Stress in higher education is therefore difficult to understand as a single problem with a single cause.

Pandemic-era research adds useful context while also requiring careful interpretation. Mushquash and Grassia (2021) found an association between COVID-related stress and depressive symptoms, while Zurlo et al. (2020) identified student-specific pandemic stressors such as academic disruption and isolation. These findings should not be treated as evidence that all contemporary student stress is pandemic-related. Rather, they show how academic stress can intensify when students simultaneously face uncertainty, disrupted routines, and reduced social support.

Stress and Assessment Performance

The relationship between stress and performance is especially important when universities rely on high-stakes examinations to evaluate learning. Heissel et al. (2021) measured cortisol during high-stakes testing and found that students with stronger physiological stress responses performed substantially below what their prior achievement would have predicted. The reported difference was approximately 0.40 standard deviations.

This result should not be interpreted to mean that stress matters more than preparation or that examination scores are invalid in every case. It does, however, provide evidence that acute stress can meaningfully influence performance even after prior achievement is taken into account. A test score may therefore reflect both what a student knows and how that student responds to the testing environment.

That distinction matters for assessment validity. If the goal of an examination is to measure knowledge or skill, universities should consider whether unnecessary features of the assessment environment are introducing additional variance unrelated to the learning objective. This does not require removing deadlines, standards, or difficult material. It requires asking whether the method used to measure learning is aligned as closely as possible with the learning itself.

Approaches to Reducing the Effects of Stress

Research points to several possible approaches, including changes to assessment design, greater access to mental health support, and instruction in coping strategies. The evidence does not identify a single intervention that will work for every student, and some approaches remain preliminary. Taken together, however, the literature suggests that universities can influence at least some of the conditions that shape student stress.

Assessment Design

Fritts and Marszalek (2010) examined computerized adaptive testing, anxiety, and gender and found that testing format and anxiety can interact differently across students. Their findings do not establish adaptive testing as a universal solution to student stress. They do suggest, however, that assessment design can influence the experience of testing and that the effects of a particular format may vary across populations.

This has broader implications for university assessment. Rather than assuming that one testing format is equally appropriate for everyone, institutions can evaluate whether the structure, stakes, timing, and frequency of assessments affect students’ ability to demonstrate learning. Lower-stakes assessments, opportunities for feedback, and multiple opportunities to demonstrate mastery are reasonable directions for further study, but the evidence reviewed here does not establish any one of these approaches as a complete solution.

Coping Skills and Mental Health Support

Individual coping strategies also matter. Mushquash and Grassia (2021) reported that students using more active coping strategies experienced less distress during the pandemic. This finding suggests that universities may be able to help students by teaching practical stress-management skills through counseling services, wellness programming, or integration into existing courses.

At the same time, coping instruction should not be treated as a substitute for institutional change. Teaching a student how to manage stress will have limited effect if that student continues to face overwhelming workload, financial instability, inaccessible counseling, or other persistent pressures. The most useful role for coping interventions may therefore be as one component of a broader support system rather than as the primary solution.

Predictive Technology

Infanta et al. (2026) explored the use of machine-learning models to identify patterns in student stress data. In principle, predictive tools could help universities recognize students who may need support before academic problems become more severe. At present, however, such approaches remain early-stage and raise practical questions about privacy, consent, data security, institutional capacity, and the consequences of incorrectly identifying students as “at risk.”

For that reason, predictive technology should be treated as a developing research area rather than an established intervention. Any future implementation would need to balance potential benefits with clear protections for students and careful consideration of how predictions are used.

Equity and Structural Conditions

Student stress also raises questions of equity because the conditions that produce stress are not distributed evenly. Financial pressure, employment demands, limited access to support, and uncertainty about basic resources can make academic responsibilities more difficult to manage (Aloka, 2024). Students with greater financial or social resources may have more flexibility to respond to the same academic demands.

This means that interventions focused entirely on individual coping are unlikely to address every source of stress. Universities should also examine policies and resources related to financial support, food security, workload, counseling access, and academic flexibility. More research is needed to determine how these institutional conditions interact with stress and academic performance across different student populations.

The equity question is therefore not whether particular groups are inherently less capable of managing stress. It is whether students are being asked to meet the same academic expectations under meaningfully different material and psychological conditions.

Future Directions

The literature suggests several priorities for future research. First, the relationship between stress and assessment validity deserves further study. Heissel et al. (2021) provide evidence that physiological stress responses can be associated with lower performance even after prior achievement is considered. This does not prove that high-stakes testing should be abandoned, but it does justify closer examination of whether alternative assessment structures can preserve rigor while reducing stress-related distortion.

Second, future work should more directly examine structural sources of stress. Research on coping strategies is valuable, but it cannot answer questions about how financial insecurity, employment, food access, course design, or institutional support shape students’ academic experiences. Studies that compare individual-level and institution-level interventions could help universities determine where limited resources have the greatest effect.

Third, more longitudinal research is needed. Linden et al. (2022) demonstrate the value of following students across an academic year rather than measuring stress at a single moment. Similar studies across multiple semesters could determine whether interventions produce lasting improvements or only temporary reductions in distress.

Finally, researchers should evaluate interventions across diverse student populations. Fritts and Marszalek’s (2010) findings indicate that assessment-related anxiety may not operate identically across groups. A solution that benefits one group of students may have smaller effects—or different effects—for another. Institutional policies should therefore be evaluated for both overall effectiveness and variation in outcomes across students.

Conclusion

Stress in higher education is not simply a matter of students feeling uncomfortable. Persistent stress can affect mental health, and acute physiological stress can influence performance in high-stakes academic settings (Heissel et al., 2021; Linden et al., 2022; Mushquash & Grassia, 2021). At the same time, the evidence does not support a single universal solution.

The strongest path forward is likely a combination of approaches: assessment practices that are carefully aligned with learning goals, accessible mental health support, practical coping resources, and attention to the structural conditions that place some students under greater pressure than others. Universities do not need to remove rigor to take stress seriously. They need to ensure that academic performance reflects learning as accurately as possible rather than avoidable differences in students’ ability to tolerate stress.
