Ecofeminism, Social Ecology, and Bioregionalism

James Machado

Philosophy
University of Oregon

Philosophy September 2026 7 min read

Ecofeminism and the Logic of Domination

Why do we degrade nature to the degree that we do? Nature suffers at the expense of humanity’s selfish needs. People defile and abuse land in the name of furthering their own goals. Ecofeminism argues that this destruction for the purpose of achieving human goals is deeply rooted in the oppression of women. Within patriarchal systems, men have historically been associated with reason, culture, and transcendence over nature, while women have been associated with nature, the body, reproduction, and care work (Mellor, 2005).

The ecofeminist argument is grounded in the idea that environmental destruction cannot be separated from systems of social inequality. Both women and nature have been treated as resources to be controlled, exploited, and dominated. Social relationships must therefore be reimagined so that they do not simply consist of a dichotomy between the dominated and the dominator, but instead exist in ecological balance.

Murray Bookchin captures this broader idea when he concludes that “the way human beings deal with each other as social beings is crucial to addressing the ecological crisis” (Bookchin, 1993, p. 462). At the center of modern environmental destruction, Bookchin identifies “economic, ethnic, cultural, and gender conflicts” as contributing to ecological devastation (Bookchin, 1993, p. 462). Systems of social hierarchy, characterized by themes of dominion, fundamentally shape human interactions with nature. The way society treats people is inseparable from the way humanity treats the natural world. A persistent desire for dominion within human society, particularly in the domination of women, can therefore extend into ecological exploitation.

Materialist Ecofeminism and Social Power

A core value of ecofeminism is its connection between environmental issues and political systems. Rather than making the oversimplified claim that women are “naturally closer” to nature, Mellor’s approach to materialist ecofeminism is considerably more complex. She argues that caregiving, domestic labor, and reproduction have historically placed women into social roles that mediate the relationship between humanity and nature. Capitalist systems rely heavily on the often invisible labor of women, just as they rely on the exploitation of nature.

As a result, environmental degradation can benefit those with social and economic power while disproportionately harming marginalized groups, particularly women. When viewed through the lens of Bookchin’s philosophy, this argument becomes increasingly concrete. As Bookchin writes, “Hierarchical mentality and class relationships that so thoroughly permeate society give rise to the very idea of dominating the natural world” (Bookchin, 1993, p. 462).

Ecofeminism creates an additional dimension to this argument by specifically incorporating the persistent domination of women into the equation. It emphasizes that the systems outlined by Bookchin frequently associate women with nature, emotion, and domestic labor. This association can enable the domination of both women and nature by men who benefit from masculine privilege within these systems of control.

Ecofeminism therefore expands environmental ethics beyond one-dimensional and often abstract discussions of “nature” and into the realm of domination that has embedded itself within society.

Bioregionalism and the Problem of Access

An important tension subsequently emerges between ecofeminism and bioregionalism, particularly the form of bioregionalism articulated by Kirkpatrick Sale. Sale emphasizes that humans should organize themselves around the local, live from and reconnect with the land, and orient society around natural regions rather than boundaries created by industrial or political systems (Sale, 1985).

While seemingly environmentally conscious, this vision can overlook the social and economic privileges required to realistically participate in such a lifestyle. Living locally in the manner Sale describes may depend on access to land, wealth, education, and stable communities. Although Sale’s ideas remain important and relevant to consider, his argument contains limitations that both Bookchin’s social ecology and ecofeminist thought help illuminate.

In his exposition of social ecology, Bookchin argues against ecological solutions that focus too heavily on individual behavioral changes while ignoring the larger systemic forces responsible for society’s misaligned relationship with nature. Solutions cannot come merely from “personalistic forms of consumption” or “green capitalism,” but instead require a much broader transformation of hierarchical and domination-based structures (Bookchin, 1993, p. 464).

From an ecofeminist perspective, Sale’s theory risks romanticizing a return to locality by omitting the inequalities that shape who can realistically participate in that return. Rather than escaping the social hierarchies of the industrial society it seeks to criticize, bioregionalism could unintentionally reproduce some of those same inequalities within its proposed solution.

Where Ecofeminism and Bioregionalism Meet

Nonetheless, Sale’s bioregionalism and Mellor’s ecofeminism are not entirely different. They have several important points of intersection, including a shared rejection of the contemporary tendency to disengage and divorce humanity from nature while simultaneously criticizing ingrained systems of industrial domination and exploitation.

Sale’s ideas about reconnecting humans to local places and recognizing the limits of nature support the ecofeminist concept that humanity is embedded within the natural world. Ecofeminism, however, goes further by insisting upon the transformation of patriarchy, labor exploitation, and unequal power relations.

These changes are vital to the ecofeminist response to systems of domination, where relocation and lifestyle changes alone fall short of eliminating the underlying structures that produce inequality.

Toward a More Complete Ecological Ethics

Ecofeminism therefore offers a compelling response to the ecological crisis by presenting an environmental philosophy that challenges a number of other proposed remedies. Rather than viewing environmental problems as isolated within nature itself, ecofeminism recognizes that ecological crises are inseparable from social crises. It examines how systems of domination shape both human relationships and humanity’s relationship with nature.

Bookchin’s critique of capitalism’s imperative toward continual growth further strengthens the ecofeminist position. Within economic systems organized around expansion, environmental destruction can come to be treated as a necessity rather than as an unavoidable consequence of human existence. Humanity can pursue development and collective goals without necessarily doing so at the expense of the natural world.

Without discrediting other environmental approaches such as bioregionalism, which offer credible yet incomplete critiques of industrial society, ecofeminism develops a framework that addresses not only the symptoms of ecological destruction but also the underlying structures of power that make such destruction possible in the first place.
