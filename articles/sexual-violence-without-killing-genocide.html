Is Sexual Violence Without Killing Sufficient to Constitute Genocide?

Adam Salim

Law & Criminal Justice September 2026 11 min read

Introduction: Defining the Question

Rape has long been recognised as an illegitimate practice during warfare and explicitly condemned at various points in history. As early as the 16th century, Gentili established just-war traditions that unequivocally condemned rape as unlawful behaviour, describing it as an act that violated the moral limits governing warfare (Gentili, 2009; Vergerio, 2017). Similarly, in The Social Contract, Rousseau (1762) argued that war is a conflict between states rather than individuals and that non-combatant civilians, men and women alike, should therefore not be considered legitimate targets of violence. The Lieber Code (1863), the first military code regulating the conduct of US soldiers in war, also explicitly condemned rape and treated it as an offence punishable by execution (Gesley, 2018).

This centuries-long consensus establishes rape as a recognised violation of the laws of war. Yet its persistence despite that consensus suggests that, in some conflicts, it serves a function beyond opportunistic violence and requires further examination. This raises a definitional question with tangible legal stakes: can sexual violence alone, in the absence of mass killing, constitute genocide? This paper argues that it can, especially when such violence operates as a coordinated instrument of group destruction rather than as incidental wartime violence—a distinction the case of Foča makes particularly clear.

Sexual Violence as an Instrument of Group Destruction

The case of Prosecutor v. Akayesu (1998) established this possibility in international criminal law by recognising rape and sexual violence as acts capable of constituting genocide when committed as a means of group destruction during the genocide against the Tutsis in Rwanda (Amann, 1999). This legal recognition reflects a broader pattern that predates its formal codification.

Brownmiller (1975, p. 38) elaborates on the nature of rape when applied to mass atrocities: “the body of a raped woman becomes a ceremonial battlefield… a message passed between men—vivid proof of victory for one and loss and defeat for the other.” In this sense, rape can become a symbol of national conquest, with the violation of individual bodies representing the domination of a wider community.

Such dynamics were present among German troops accused of raping Belgian women during the 1914 “Rape of Belgium,” after which propaganda circulated that weaponised these accounts to create a sense of dishonour and national humiliation among enemies (Curry, 2022). This earlier case illustrates rape's symbolic and hegemonic functions—humiliation, territorial domination, and subjugation—but it does not by itself establish genocide, because violence against a national enemy is not equivalent to an attempt to destroy that group as such.

Foča's rape campaign, in which Bosniak women were systematically subjected to sexual violence, shares this hegemonic logic but goes further. Applying Straus' (2006) definition of genocide, which emphasises group selectivity and group destruction rather than physical extermination alone, the campaign can be understood as genocidal where the violence targeted the biological and social reproduction of the group.

Ideology, Reproduction, and the Foča Campaign

This intent was first authorised ideologically. Serb commanders and politicians endorsed the violence through racist mythology. Former Bosnian Serb leader and president of Republika Srpska Biljana Plavsic portrayed Bosniaks as biologically corrupted Serbs possessing a “bad gene,” supposedly damaged by Turk-Muslim influence (Becirevic, 2014, p. 118).

Such framing positioned sexual violence as a means of biological and social destruction by attacking non-Serb social cohesion, reproductive continuity, and community security (Allen, 1996). MacKinnon (1994) describes this deployment of rape as “ethnic expansion through forced reproduction.”

This logic also aligned with influential nationalist ideologies of the period, particularly “Greater Serbia” irredentism—the aspiration to establish a Serb state incorporating territories understood as historically significant to Serbs, including areas within modern Bosnia and Herzegovina (Savić, 2014).

Yet ideology alone did not make the violence systematic. It required a bureaucratic and institutional framework through which that ideology could be implemented. Detention centres and rape camps became part of operational practice, with sexually enslaved Bosniak and Croat women held in schools, sports halls, private houses, and military facilities. Repeated assaults formed part of the wider campaign of violence.

Crucially, these acts cannot be understood solely as disorganised violence committed by isolated individuals. Local authorities, police officials, and political actors cooperated in supervising facilities and providing soldiers access to enslaved women (ICTY, 2001). Testimony examined by the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia indicated that soldiers were ordered to commit sexual violence and acted within propaganda and revenge narratives (Askin, 1997).

Explanations attributed to perpetrators included attempts to “improv[e] their fighting spirit” and claims that women should be “inseminated by the Serb seed,” reflecting the supremacist and reproductive logic surrounding the violence (Becirevic, 2014, pp. 122–123). Within this framework, violence targeted not only individuals but the continuity and identity of Bosniak and Croat communities.

From Ideological Permission to Institutional Violence

The combination of elite ideological authorisation and local institutional compliance is partly captured by Kiernan's (2007) concept of “Cults of Antiquity.” Kiernan identifies the romanticisation of mythical pasts and ideals of ethnic purity as recurring features of nationalist identity that regimes can mobilise to generate public support.

This dynamic is visible in Bosnian Serb rhetoric, where historical mythology and dehumanising portrayals of non-Serb populations could be used to legitimise or normalise violence. Kiernan further argues that such movements are not necessarily driven by genuine cultural preservation but can emerge as reactions to perceived social decay or decline.

However, an account centred only on elite ideology cannot explain why local authorities and ordinary soldiers participated at scale. The evidence from Foča provides another part of that explanation. Coordination between police officials, camp administrators, political authorities, and combat units demonstrates that systematic sexual violence depended not only on ideological permission from above but also on sustained cooperation by actors capable of identifying victims, operating facilities, and enforcing access to them.

Sexual violence, in other words, became potentially genocidal not through racist ideology alone, nor through isolated acts of violence, but through the fusion of the two: an ideology of biological and social destruction made operational through institutions willing to carry it out. It is this relationship between group-directed intent and coordinated implementation that satisfies Straus' conceptual threshold for genocide and that a narrowly death-centred definition risks overlooking.

Social Death and the Genocide Threshold

If genocide, as defined by Straus, can be carried out through sexual violence, a further question follows: can such violence be sufficient in the absence of mass killing?

Card (2003) argues that genocide should not be understood exclusively through its biological or homicidal dimensions. Instead, she emphasises the destruction of the intergenerational relationships that shape collective identity and give meaning to human lives—a process she describes through the concept of “social death.”

Social death refers to the destruction of the communal vitality of a collective: the traditions, institutions, relationships, and practices through which a group continues to exist socially. By “natally alienating” the descendants of victims, perpetrators can disrupt the intergenerational transmission of culture and identity without necessarily requiring the physical death of every member of the group (Patterson, 1982).

This framework places the rape campaign in Foča in a different light. Even in the absence of mass killing, the systematic targeting of reproductive continuity, family structures, and communal cohesion could inflict precisely the form of social destruction that Card identifies as central to genocide.

There is, however, a risk in broadening the concept too far. Including collective social and emotional destruction within the definition can stretch the legal and conceptual boundaries of genocide (Fein, 1993). If genocide becomes entirely detached from physical destruction, the term risks losing the specificity that distinguishes it from other serious international crimes, including crimes against humanity and war crimes, each of which carries its own legal framework.

This concern is not merely theoretical. The ICTY's Kunarac judgement, which directly addressed crimes committed during the Foča campaign, convicted the defendants of rape and enslavement as crimes against humanity rather than genocide (ICTY, 2001). This illustrates the caution international courts exercise when applying the genocide label and the demanding evidentiary threshold attached to genocidal intent.

That legal restraint is defensible within the requirements of criminal prosecution. However, a court's decision not to convict for genocide does not necessarily resolve the broader conceptual question of how a campaign of violence should be understood. Card's framework is therefore useful not as a replacement for the legal definition but as an account of what may make genocide morally distinctive as a phenomenon.

From this perspective, describing Foča only through a lesser legal category does not eliminate the underlying definitional tension. Bosniak and Croat women were targeted as members of communities, while sexual violence was connected to efforts to damage those groups' biological, familial, and social continuity. Whether that evidence satisfies the legal threshold for genocide in a particular prosecution remains a separate question from whether the campaign reflects the destructive logic that the concept of genocide is intended to identify.

Conclusion

Sexual violence does not become genocide simply because it occurs during war, nor does every systematic campaign of rape necessarily satisfy the definition. The distinction rests on the purpose the violence serves and the group-directed intent behind it.

The case of Foča demonstrates why mass killing should not automatically be treated as the only form through which a group can be destroyed. When sexual violence is connected to an ideology of ethnic domination, directed toward a protected group, and implemented through coordinated institutions in ways that attack reproductive continuity, family relationships, and communal identity, the distinction between sexual violence as a war crime and sexual violence as an instrument of genocide becomes considerably more difficult to maintain.

The legal threshold for genocide should remain demanding because of the specificity and gravity of the offence. At the same time, an exclusively death-centred understanding risks overlooking campaigns designed to destroy a group's capacity to reproduce itself biologically and socially. Foča therefore exposes a tension between legal caution and conceptual understanding: genocide may involve killing, but group destruction need not always depend upon it.
