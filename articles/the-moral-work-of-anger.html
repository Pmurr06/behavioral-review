The Moral Work of Anger

Braydon Perko

Business Administration
University of Oregon

Behavioral Science July 2026 7 min read

  5. References

Public conversations today tend to push people toward calm. Anger gets framed as something dangerous, a threat to clear thinking rather than a pathway toward justice. But this view misses something crucial. Throughout history, anger has dragged wrongdoing into the open. It has made people speak when staying quiet would have been far easier. And it has shoved society, sometimes kicking and screaming, toward genuine transformation. What matters isn't simply that someone feels angry. Why they feel it, that's the real question. Getting furious because someone cuts you off in traffic? That's one thing. But anger that accumulates after years of racism, sexism, political deception, or systemic injustice? That's something else entirely. This second type runs deep. It grows from actual harm. When you examine it honestly, anger starts functioning almost like a flashlight, illuminating problems that those in power would prefer to minimize or ignore altogether. And here's the thing: anger mobilizes. It pushes people to resist, to organize, to march, to demand better, instead of pretending everything is fine when clearly it isn't. And honestly, without that push, many of the turning points we take for granted today might never have happened.

Anger as a Moral Response

Philosophers like Srinivasan sharpen this distinction. She argues anger can be "apt" even when it fails strategically or wins no arguments. The more you sit with that idea, the more sense it makes. We usually judge anger by whether it works. But that standard isn't fair, and honestly, it mostly benefits people invested in keeping things unchanged. Sometimes anger is simply the correct response because something genuinely wrong occurred. It doesn't need to be tactical to be legitimate. When someone faces exclusion or mistreatment, their anger signals that harm is real. Telling them to settle down or act "reasonable" typically just shields the comfort of whoever holds more power. Understood this way, anger stops being merely an emotion. It becomes a mechanism for identifying and naming injustice, particularly the kind people work hard to downplay.

Anger in a Post-Truth Society

This matters even more in what McIntyre calls a "post-truth" culture. Post-truth isn't just disagreement over facts. It's facts themselves becoming optional. People grab onto whatever "truth" fits their identity or what they emotionally prefer believing. Misinformation spreads with terrifying ease under these conditions. Accountability becomes slippery. During COVID-19, conspiracy theories and vaccine falsehoods outpaced scientific information. Experts weren't rejected for being wrong. They were rejected because their findings clashed with what certain groups wanted to hear. Anger from doctors, nurses, journalists, families who lost people they loved, that anger forced attention onto human consequences: preventable deaths, unequal risks, leaders exploiting confusion for political gain.

We've witnessed this pattern beyond the pandemic. In elections where voter suppression occurred, anger drove communities to action. People launched fact-checking efforts. They helped voters understand their rights. They knocked on doors and corrected misinformation online in real time. This wasn't impulsive or irrational anger. It was a natural response to deliberate deception and exclusion. Rather than creating disorder, it restored some clarity to a muddied situation. Anger like this isn't merely complaining. It's refusing to accept a warped version of reality as truth.

History, Justice, and Social Change

Anger has performed this clarifying function throughout history. Audre Lorde speaks about it directly. She argues that people facing racism or sexism shouldn't be expected to suppress their anger or apologize for it. She calls anger "a powerful source of energy" that drags crucial issues into visibility (Lorde 3). Consider the Civil Rights Movement. The anger people carried after decades of segregation, violence, and degradation wasn't chaotic or aimless. It had focus. It fueled sit-ins and marches and speeches and court battles and countless acts of resistance that eventually pushed the country toward actual change. "Every woman has a well-stocked arsenal of anger potentially useful against those oppressions," Lorde writes (Lorde 3). That anger didn't fracture the movement. It bound it together.

Similar patterns emerge elsewhere in history. The suffrage movement drew power from generations of frustration at being denied political voice. Labor unions grew from rage at dangerous conditions and stolen wages. Even the American Revolution, so often romanticized, was propelled by genuine outrage, fury at exploitation, at taxation without representation, at being treated as lesser subjects. Anger doesn't materialize from nothing in these situations. It builds as people recognize they deserve more.

Nussbaum contributes something valuable here. She suggests anger can transform into "forward-looking thoughts," meaning it doesn't have to remain stuck on revenge (Nussbaum). It can evolve into something that pursues repair and improvement. The #MeToo movement demonstrates exactly this. Survivors weren't just angry at specific individuals. They were angry at entire systems that had shielded abuse for decades. Their anger produced policy reforms, forced resignations, sparked public conversations about harassment, and created accountability where none had existed. This is precisely the anger Nussbaum describes: anger that begins by naming wrongs and concludes by rebuilding something better.

Worth noting too, anger doesn't appear only in massive national movements. Often it shows up in smaller, everyday moments that still carry weight. Workers demanding fair pay or safer conditions. Students protesting policies that feel unjust. Local communities pushing back against harmful policing or housing decisions. Anger surfaces in all these contexts. These smaller acts won't appear in history textbooks, but they follow identical patterns. Anger wakes people up. It stops them from accepting what they would normally just tolerate. So when people claim anger is "unproductive," they're overlooking countless everyday instances where anger is precisely what finally makes someone stand up and act.

Critics still insist anger poses dangers, of course. They argue it divides communities, escalates conflict, complicates compromise. And yes, anger can get messy. Movements sometimes derail through internal disputes. Online arguments spiral into chaos. But none of this proves anger itself is the problem. Srinivasan notes that even when anger fails strategically, that doesn't diminish its moral appropriateness. Lorde puts it more bluntly: "My fear of anger taught me nothing. Your fear of that anger will teach you nothing, also" (Lorde 1). Movements don't collapse because people got angry. They collapse when that anger gets ignored or dismissed. When anger carries purpose and direction, as in the Civil Rights Movement or #MeToo, it becomes a force pushing society forward rather than holding it back.

Conclusion

Anger gets labeled irrational or unproductive constantly. But that framing completely misses the moral work anger accomplishes. Sometimes anger is the only honest reaction to injustice. In a world saturated with misinformation and confusion, anger cuts through noise. And this isn't merely historical. You see anger powering movements right now: environmental justice protests, disability rights activism, youth-led climate strikes, marches against gun violence. Young people especially hear they're "too emotional." In reality, they're responding to problems they didn't create. Their anger isn't theatrical. It signals that ignoring issues solves nothing.

History demonstrates repeatedly that anger isn't just an emotion. It's a catalyst. The anger of abolitionists, suffragists, civil rights leaders, labor organizers, survivors of abuse, it transformed society in ways calmness alone never could have. Anger compels attention when people would rather look away. It disrupts comfort. It drives action, sometimes imperfect, sometimes chaotic, but necessary nonetheless. If a society genuinely wants progress, it cannot treat anger as something to avoid. It must take anger seriously, examine where it originates, and actually listen to what it reveals. Ignoring anger has always delayed progress. Listening to it has opened doors for change.
