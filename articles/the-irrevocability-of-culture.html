The Irrevocability of Culture

Rez Ball (Sydney Freeland, 2024)

James Machado

Philosophy
University of Oregon

Philosophy August 2026 9 min read

The 2024 film Rez Ball, directed by Sydney Freeland is much more than just a typical underdog sports story. The central story of the film follows the Chuska Warriors, a high school basketball team on a Navajo reservation in New Mexico with a singular goal of winning the state championship. However, this film delves much deeper into the meaning behind this. For those in Chuska, basketball is much more than just a game. It is the foundation of their community and culture, and when tragedy strikes in this film, basketball and Navajo culture come together to remedy the grief. By specifically manipulating the focus and perspective, implementing real basketball strategy, and by utilizing deep cultural symbols, the film posits a question that is prevalent in many sports movies. What is winning, and what is the value of that winning? This essay argues that for the Chuska Warriors, culture is not the only reason for their success, but the fundamental base behind their winning. Moreover, winning, in the context of this film, is turning insurmountable loss and unimaginable struggle into victory, not just for the Chuska basketball program, but for the entire culture of the Navajo Indians.

One of the most important film thinking strategies the film utilizes is focus and perspective. The most obvious example of this is the way in which the camera focuses on the crowd in the midst of action shots of the games. This occurs in each game, as in between shots of Jimmy hitting a three or Coach Hobbs orchestrating the offense, we see shots of the Navajo supporters. For example, during the championship game at the University of New Mexico's infamous arena “The Pit,” the camera consistently fades the focus from a Navajo man and his traditional hat to the rest of Chuska cheering closer to the court. This is a very intentional film thinking choice, as the movie seeks to emphasize that winning here resides in the fact that the people of Chuska are present, resilient, and part of a larger community identity entirely inseparable from that of the team.

Another way in which Rez Ball employs a unique form of perspective is when our perspective as the audience shifts from Nataanii to Jimmy. Although Jimmy is the main character in the film, Nataanii is constantly present in the preliminary shots of the movie. Because of this, the viewers are subjected to feel as though Nataanii is the main character, considering he is the one experiencing the tragic loss of his mother and sister, and that he is the star player on the basketball team. It is only after his death that we realize that the protagonist is Jimmy. This is a shocking shift, and the emotional weight of death by suicide forces the audience to feel the significance of this shift. Freeland here is willing to go to dark places in order to demonstrate to the audience the reality of winning. Jimmy must shoulder this burden of loss and overcome the adversity he faces through it. As noted in the process of editing this essay, the obstacle of grief that Jimmy faces becomes the pathway to victory. Jimmy must lead his program through this obstacle through the culture and togetherness of his team, proving that winning and culture are entirely intertwined.

Unlike many Hollywood sports movies, Rez Ball does not employ the use of body doubles in any of the action sequences. Instead, Freeland made the intentional choice of hiring actors who had actual real basketball experience. Furthermore, as a basketball player myself, I noticed how many of the game scenes featured real basketball plays, such as when Gallup runs a classic “horns” action. The inclusion of these subtle details contribute to the overall realism and authenticity of the film, serving to engross the viewers in the subject of this film. In terms of actual film thinking elements, the film manipulates speed during big plays. For example, when Chuska is playing Los Lunas after having herded the sheep and decided that they will call all their plays in Navajo, the speed shifts. As the game enters clutch time, the entire play is in slow motion as they execute the offense they called in Navajo. The Chuska players are communicating in Navajo as they run the offense, and the sound of the crowd is overtaken by Native music, drumming, and singing. The changes in pace isolate the complexity, and therefore the focus and skill needed to play “rez ball.” Moreover, the culturally based adaptations that the Warriors made in conjunction with this style of play denote not only the vital importance of basketball to Chuska, but its deep ties to Native American identity, the reason why they win.

The most important theme of the movie is culture, and the value that culture creates. Not just the culture of the Chuska basketball program, but how that intersects with the culture of the town of Chuska and that of the larger Navajo nation. The significance of this culture is captured in the moment when Coach Hobbs is running near the bridge after winning the state championship. She receives a couple of voice messages, the first two from two prestigious Division 1 basketball programs who seek her out after the state championship win. The third comes from her former partner, Alena, congratulating her for the win, coupled with the phrase “looks like the res suits you” (Freeland, 2024, 1:42:31). Looking out over the rocks on the reservation in the distance, Hobbs takes a deep breath and responds “one more season” (Freeland, 2024, 1:42:47). Here, the film is thinking about the ultimate value of the profession of coaching, and what it really means to win at any sport. Among elite basketball programs and coaches, culture is not created or established just for the sake of wins. If you were to ask great Naismith Hall of Fame coaches such as Tom Izzo or Pat Summit, the culture of one's program is the end goal. The true purpose of coaching at any level is to develop young men or women into contributing members of society through unilateral standards of respect, toughness, humility, and relentless effort. Winning, of course, is a vital byproduct of these practices, however the value of that winning is providing athletes with a set of tools to combat adversity throughout their lives, long after they leave the court. Returning to Hobbs, by committing to another season when offered superior jobs that most coaches would accept in a heartbeat, she is acknowledging that she has found a community where the ultimate goal of her profession can be achieved, and with greater importance than almost anywhere else in the world. The Chuska culture is more than just the development of young men and women through basketball. Basketball is the life blood of the reservation, the very heartbeat of Navajo heritage that represents this culture's propensity for resilience. By choosing to stay here, Hobbs is recognizing that winning for the Chuska Warriors is entirely indistinguishable from instilling the values outlined above into a generation that “always find[s] a way to lose” (Freeland, 2024, 31:52). In this way, the irreplaceable culture of Chuska is not simply a tool used to win championships. Actually, it is quite the opposite. The championship is the evidence of a culture that has successfully taught its players how to exhibit the exceptional and extraordinary values of resilience and togetherness. The values of Rez Ball.

The most powerful scene in the movie happens at the free throw line during the championship. By placing Jimmy in a spotlight and darkening everything else in the rest of the scene, the movie isolates Jimmy from the rest of the gym. This symbolizes how he felt throughout the movie, dealing with the loss of his teammate, the team's leader, and his best friend. It symbolizes his isolation, as he gets little to no support from his mother, and how he has had to not only shoulder this loss but somehow lead his team out of it through the immense pressure that his entire community places on him as the star basketball player. Then, his mother appears, sitting in a lawn chair on the baseline just as she was at the beginning of the movie, reminding Jimmy that Indians “alway[s] find a way to lose.” Subsequently, he misses the second free throw. The isolation disappears. He looks up into the crowd to see Nataani's name on a huge poster, and his team, the entire faction of Chuska supporting him. He retorts back to his mother “only one way to find out,” then sinks the third and final free throw to bring the championship home to Chuska (Freeland, 2024, 1:39:12). This scene is a battle between two ideas, two identities. His mother represents all the negative stereotypes and hardships all Navajo Indians have been forced to endure for centuries. Jimmy's response, however, is an act of pride. By sinking the shot in defiance of his mother's words, he proves that his culture is not a source of weakness, that Indians are not destined to lose, but rather that the Navajo way is a source of strength, of power, and is not the reason for past failures.

In conclusion, Rez Ball is arguing that winning, and the value that it brings, truly has nothing to do with winning at all. Although winning the state championship is a central part of the movie, it is a vehicle utilized by Sydney Freeland to reclaim and define the Navajo identity. By using film thinking elements such as the shift in perspective from the game to the crowd, to the manipulation of speed and intentional injection of Native music, sounds, and language into the rez ball brand of basketball, to the isolation of Jimmy in the game winning scene, the movie is thinking about how the quality of culture is the real achievement. For the town of Chuska and the Warriors, winning is simply the fruit of their collective ability to withstand adversity. In line with greats such as Pat Summit and Tom Izzo, Coach Hobbs willingness to stay and continue to coach is evidence of this resilience, and the culture of the Navajo people that breeds human excellence. For the Chuska Warriors, the win of the state championship is the not the light at the end of the tunnel or the pot of gold at the end of the rainbow, it is the standard that they constantly exceed thanks to the Chuska and Navajo culture whose definition resides in resilience, togetherness, and the relentlessness of Rez Ball.
